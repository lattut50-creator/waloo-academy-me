4.1 Classification of Natural Resources

🔹 Based on the Origin of Natural Resources

Biotic Resources

🔸Resources that have life or come from living organisms.
🔹Examples:
🔹🌳 Plants
🔹🐄 Animals
🔹🦠 Microorganisms
🔹⛽ Fossil fuels

Abiotic Resources

🔸Resources that do not have life and come from non-living things.
🔹Examples:
🔹💧 Water
🔹🌬️ Air
🔹🌱 Soil
🔹🪨 Rocks
🔹💎 Minerals

🔹 Based on the Availability of Resources

Renewable Resources

🔸Resources that can be replaced or replenished naturally.
🔹They are renewed faster than they are used.
🔹Examples:
🔹☀️ Solar energy
🔹💧 Water
🔹🌬️ Wind

Non-Renewable Resources

🔸Resources that are limited and can be used up completely.
🔹They take millions of years to form.
🔹Examples:
🔹⛽ Fossil fuels
🔹🪨 Coal
🔹🦏 Rare species of organisms
📌 Slide Summary: Natural resources are classified by origin (biotic vs. abiotic) and by availability (renewable vs. non-renewable). Biotic resources come from living organisms; abiotic from non-living matter. Renewable resources replenish naturally; non-renewable resources are finite.
🌿 Chapter 4 | Slide 3
Classification of Natural Resources (Potential vs Reserved)

Potential Resources

🔸Resources that are known to exist but have not been measured completely and are not yet used.
🔹They may be used in the future.
🔹Example:
🔹🌬️ Wind energy in areas where electricity is not yet generated from wind.
🔹☢️ Nuclear minerals

Reserved Resources

🔸Resources that have been identified and measured, but are kept for future use.
🔹They are available but not currently exploited.
🔹Example:
🔹🌊 Rivers

📌 Important Difference

🔹Potential Resources → Known to exist, but not fully quantified or utilized.
🔹Reserved Resources → Already identified and quantified, but intentionally saved for future use.
📌 Slide Summary: Potential resources are known but not yet fully measured or used. Reserved resources are identified and quantified but deliberately kept for future use. The key difference is that reserved resources are quantified and intentionally saved.
🌿 Chapter 4 | Slide 4
Classification of Natural Resources (Stock vs Actual)

Stock Resources

🔸Resources that have been discovered and measured, but cannot be used currently because technology is insufficient.
🔹The resource exists, but we lack the technology to utilize it economically.
🔹Example:
🔹🧪 Hydrogen

Actual Resources

🔸Resources that have been discovered, measured, developed, and are currently being used.
🔹These resources contribute directly to production and economic activities.
🔹Examples:
🔹⛽ Crude oil
🔹🌳 Forests
📌 Slide Summary: Stock resources are discovered but unusable due to technological limitations. Actual resources are discovered, developed, and currently in use. Stock resources may become actual resources when technology advances.

🔹The environment acts as a natural sink for waste.
🔹Rivers, forests, soil, and microorganisms help break down many waste materials.

🔹People enjoy natural scenery such as:
🔹🌳 Forests

🔹🌺 Parks

Ecologically Essential

4.2.1 Theory of Optimal Depletion

Suppose the economy produces Q, which can be consumed or invested.

🔸Output Q can be used in two ways: 🍞 Consumption (now) or 🏭 Investment (future)

Output is generated through a production function using a non-renewable resource input R and manufactured capital K.

🔸R = Non-renewable resource (⛽ Oil, 🪨 Coal, 💎 Minerals)
🔸K = Manufactured capital (🏭 Factories, ⚙️ Machinery)

Production function: Q = Q(K,R)

🔸Output depends on both Capital (K) and Natural Resources (R)

Cobb-Douglas Production Function: Q = A K^α R^β

🔸Where: Q = Output, A = Technology, K = Capital, R = Resources
🔸α = Output responsiveness to capital, β = Output responsiveness to resources
🔹Both α and β are positive — more input = more output
📐 Q = A × K^α × R^β | α > 0, β > 0
📌 Slide Summary: The Theory of Optimal Depletion analyzes how non-renewable resources should be used over time. The Cobb-Douglas production function (Q = A K^α R^β) shows how both capital and resources contribute to output, with α and β measuring their contributions.
🌿 Chapter 4 | Slide 8
CES Production Function

Constant Elasticity of Substitution (CES) Production Function: Q = A (α K^{-θ} + β R^{-θ})^{-ε/θ}

🔸θ (theta) determines how easily inputs can substitute for each other
🔸Cobb-Douglas is a special case of CES when θ → 0

Essential vs Non-Essential Resources

🔸A resource is essential if Q(K,R=0) = 0 for any positive K
🔸For CD function: Both R and K are essential (Q=0 if either input=0)
🔸For CES: If θ < 0 → no input is essential (easy substitution)
🔸If θ > 0 → all inputs are essential (limited substitution)
📐 CES: Q = A(αK^{-θ} + βR^{-θ})^{-ε/θ}
📌 Slide Summary: The CES production function allows different degrees of substitution between capital and resources. Cobb-Douglas is a special case. Resources are "essential" if production stops without them. For CD, both inputs are essential; for CES, θ determines essentiality.
🌿 Chapter 4 | Slide 9
Elasticity of Substitution (σ)

The elasticity of substitution (σ) between non-renewable resources and capital measures how easily one input can replace the other while keeping output constant.

🔸Larger σ → Easier substitution
🔸Smaller σ → Harder substitution
🔸Helps understand: Resource scarcity, Technological flexibility, Long-run sustainability
📊 σ = Elasticity of Substitution = % change in (K/R) ÷ % change in (MPK/MPR)
📌 Slide Summary: Elasticity of substitution (σ) measures how easily capital can replace natural resources in production. High σ means easy substitution; low σ means difficult substitution — crucial for understanding resource scarcity and sustainability.
🌿 Chapter 4 | Slide 10
Interpreting Elasticity of Substitution (σ)

σ = 0: No input substitution possible (L-shaped isoquant, Leontief production)

🔸Inputs must be used in fixed proportions
🔸Having more of one input cannot compensate for shortage of the other

σ = ∞: Perfect substitution (straight line isoquant)

🔸One input can completely replace the other
🔸Firms can freely choose whichever input is cheaper

0 < σ < ∞: Partial substitution (convex isoquant)

🔸Most real-world production processes
🔸Lower σ → more curvature → more difficult substitution
🔸Higher σ → less curvature → easier substitution
📌 Slide Summary: σ = 0 means no substitution; σ = ∞ means perfect substitution; 0 < σ < ∞ means partial substitution. Lower σ means more difficult substitution, making resource scarcity more economically damaging.
🌿 Chapter 4 | Slide 11
Resource Scarcity Consequences

As production continues, stocks of non-renewable resources decline.

🔸Resource prices rise relative to capital prices

As relative resource price rises, resource-to-capital ratio falls.

🔸Firms substitute capital for resources when possible
🔸Marginal product of resource (MPR) increases
🔸Marginal product of capital (MPK) decreases

High σ (easy substitution):

🔸Small price changes induce large input changes
🔸Resource scarcity causes few economic problems

Low σ (difficult substitution):

🔸Even large price changes cause small input changes
🔸Resource scarcity causes serious economic damage
📌 Slide Summary: High σ means easy substitution → resource scarcity causes few problems. Low σ means difficult substitution → resource scarcity causes serious economic damage. σ = 0 means no substitution possible, making resource depletion catastrophic.
🌿 Chapter 4 | Slide 12
Feasibility of Sustainable Development

Is sustainable development actually possible?

🔸Meeting present needs without reducing future generations' ability to meet their needs

Two necessary conditions:

🔹1. A criterion of sustainability is required
🔹2. Describe material transformation conditions (production possibilities)

Sustainability Criterion: Non-declining per capita consumption over indefinite time

🔸Each generation should have at least the same consumption as the previous generation
🔸Consumption per person should not fall over time
📌 Slide Summary: Sustainable development requires (1) a clear sustainability criterion and (2) understanding of production possibilities. The chapter uses the criterion of non-declining per capita consumption over indefinite time.
🌿 Chapter 4 | Slide 13
Transformation Possibilities - Case A (Cobb-Douglas)

CASE A: Output produced under perfect competition with CD production function, constant returns to scale, inputs R and K.

🔸Without technical progress and constant population
🔸Sustainability (constant consumption) is feasible if α > β
🔹α = Capital share of output
🔹β = Resource share of output
📐 Sustainability Condition for CD: α > β
📌 Slide Summary: With Cobb-Douglas production, sustainability (constant per capita consumption) is feasible when the capital share of output (α) is greater than the resource share (β), assuming no technical progress and constant population.
🌿 Chapter 4 | Slide 14
Other Cases - CES and Backstop Technology

CASE B: CES production function, perfect competition, constant returns

🔸Without technical progress and constant population
🔸Sustainability feasible if σ = 1/(1+θ) ≥ 1

CASE C: Backstop technology permanently available

🔸Non-renewable resources are not essential
🔸Sustainability is feasible (but consumption levels may be limited)
📐 CES Condition: σ ≥ 1 | Backstop Technology: Resource not essential
📌 Slide Summary: Case B: With CES, sustainability requires elasticity of substitution σ ≥ 1. Case C: With permanent backstop technology, resources are not essential, and sustainability is feasible (though consumption may be limited).
🌿 Chapter 4 | Slide 15
Technical Progress & Population Growth Effects

Previous results assumed zero technical progress and zero population growth.

🔸Results change if either rate is non-zero

Permanent technical progress:

🔸Increases the range of circumstances where constant consumption is feasible
🔸HELPS sustainability

Constant population growth:

🔸Has the opposite effect — makes sustainability harder
🔸However, sustainability possible if technical progress is sufficiently large and β (resource share) is low
📌 Slide Summary: Technical progress helps sustainability; population growth hinders it. Even with population growth, constant per capita consumption is possible if technical progress is strong enough and the resource share is low enough.
🌿 Chapter 4 | Slide 16
General Conclusion on Sustainability

For CES production, sustained consumption is possible even where σ < 1, provided technology growth is sufficiently high relative to population growth.

The general conclusion: Sustainability requires EITHER:

🔹1. A relatively high degree of substitutability between capital and the resource (high σ)
🔹2. A sufficiently large continuing rate of technical progress
🔹3. The presence of a permanent backstop technology
🌍 SUSTAINABILITY = High σ OR Strong Technology OR Backstop Technology
📌 Chapter 4 Summary: Sustainable development requires either (1) high substitutability between capital and natural resources, (2) strong and continuous technological progress, or (3) permanent backstop technology. These three pathways can maintain consumption across generations despite resource depletion.
