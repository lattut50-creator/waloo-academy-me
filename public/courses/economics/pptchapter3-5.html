Definition of Market Concentration

Market concentration refers to the situation in which an industry or a market is controlled by a small number of leading producers who are exclusively or very largely engaged in that industry.

🔹Think of a few big companies dominating everything – like in mobile operating systems or aircraft manufacturing.
🔹"Exclusively or very largely engaged" means these top firms focus mainly on this specific industry, not as a side business.
🔹Control here means they can influence prices, supply, and competition.

It is used when smaller firms account for large percentage of the total market.

🔹Even if there are many small businesses, if a handful of them hold most of the sales, we say the market is concentrated.
🔹Example: 3 big stores + 97 tiny shops – but the 3 big ones make 80% of all sales.

It measures the extent of domination of sales by one or more firms in a particular market.

🔹It answers the question: "How much of the market's total sales belong to the largest players?"
🔹A single number (like 70% for the top 4 firms) tells you the domination level.

Two variables that determinates such situation are: The number and Size distribution of firms within the market.

🔹"Determinates" here means "determine" – the number of firms and their size distribution.
🔹First variable: How many firms exist? Fewer firms → higher concentration.
🔹Second variable: Are the firms similar in size, or one giant and many dwarfs? Unequal size → higher concentration.
📌 Key Exam Point: Market Concentration = Degree to which a few firms control sales in a market. It mainly depends on: (1) Number of firms, and (2) Size (market share) of firms.
Chapter 3 | Slide 2
Importance of Market Concentration in Industrial Economics

Having vital consequences for the pricing and output decisions of the firm, how these two dimensions cause different forms of the market structure.

🔹The number of firms and their size distribution affect how firms behave in the market.
🔹These factors influence: pricing decisions, output decisions, and market structure.

The implication of market concentration in industrial economics is far wider than theory of the firm.

🔹It's not just about one company's behavior – it affects entire industries, government policy, and antitrust laws.
🔹"Theory of the firm" focuses on a single company. Market concentration looks at the whole playing field.

Market concentration (the degree of sellers' concentration in the market), is an important element of the market structure, which plays a dominant role in determining the behaviour of a firm in the market.

🔹Concentration tells you how much power sellers have.
🔹Market structure = the environment firms operate in. That environment shapes whether firms compete fiercely or cooperate quietly.
📌 Example: One electricity provider in a town → High concentration → More control over prices. Many electricity providers → Low concentration → Higher competition → Better prices and services.
📌 Key Exam Point: Market concentration is an important element of market structure because it influences firms' pricing decisions, output decisions, and overall market behavior.
Chapter 3 | Slide 3
More About Market Concentration

A market is said to be more concentrated, the fewer the number of firms.

🔹Simple rule: 2 firms → more concentrated than 100 firms (if market shares are similar within each case).
🔹Think of a tug-of-war: fewer teams means each team pulls harder.

The "market structure-conduct-performance" requires a means of identifying different market structures: Concentration in the ownership of the industry, Concentration of decision-making power, and Concentration of the firms in a particular location or region, etc.

🔹The SCP model says: Structure → Conduct → Performance. But first you must identify the structure.
🔹Three ways concentration appears: Ownership, Decision-making power, and Geographic clustering.

All of these elements of market concentration may have considerable impact on the market performance of the firms such as profitability, growth, and technological progress.

🔹High concentration can lead to higher profits (less price competition).
🔹It can also affect how fast firms grow and whether they invest in new technology.
📊 Number of Firms vs Concentration:
100 firms → Low | 10 firms → Medium | 2 firms → High | 1 firm → Very High (Monopoly)
📌 Key Exam Point: A market is more concentrated when fewer firms control it. Market concentration affects profitability, growth, and technological progress and is an important component of the Structure-Conduct-Performance (SCP) approach.
Chapter 3 | Slide 4
Measuring Market Concentration

Measuring Market Concentration – This help us to test empirically the behavioral hypothesis about the firms and industries.

🔹"Empirically" means using real-world data, not just theory.
🔹A hypothesis like "firms in concentrated markets collude" can be tested by measuring concentration and observing behavior.

Various quantitative indices have been suggested for this purpose.

🔹Many math formulas exist to calculate concentration – each with pros and cons.
🔹Examples: Concentration Ratio (CR), Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), Gini coefficient, Entropy index.

Some of them are used to measure the monopoly power and some others for market concentration. These two terms are closely interrelated and cannot be separated from each other in the measurement process of market concentration.

🔹Monopoly power indices focus on one firm (e.g., Lerner Index).
🔹Market concentration indices look at all firms together (e.g., CR4, HHI).
🔹High market concentration usually creates potential for monopoly power.
📌 Key Exam Point: Monopoly power measures the actual power of a firm, while market concentration measures the overall dominance of firms in an industry.
Chapter 3 | Slide 5
Measures of Concentration

The measures of concentration would give us the potential monopoly power in the market or industry as a whole.

🔹Potential = what could happen if firms cooperate.
🔹A highly concentrated market has high potential for abuse, even if no firm is currently misbehaving.

Market concentration is therefore a necessary condition for the monopoly power.

🔹Necessary = you cannot have significant monopoly power without concentration.
🔹But concentration alone is not enough – you also need barriers to entry and collusion.

A market concentration measure should be decreasing as the number of firms in the market keeps on increasing.

🔹If new firms enter, concentration should drop – unless the new firm is huge.
🔹Example: 10 firms → 11 firms (all small) lowers concentration.

There are several measures suggested for measuring market concentration all of which are not equally good or bad. There is no perfect or the best index for concentration or monopoly power.

🔹No single index is perfect for every situation.
🔹Researchers choose based on their question, data availability, and industry context.
📌 Key Exam Point: Market concentration is a necessary condition for monopoly power, and concentration generally decreases as the number of firms increases.
Chapter 3 | Slide 6
Conditions of a Good Concentration Measure

A measure of market concentration tries to transform the information on the number, size and distribution of firms presented on the concentration curve into a single value.

🔹The concentration curve is a picture (graph). An index condenses that picture into one number.

Some of the general conditions that should be satisfied by each one of the indices are: The measure must yield unambiguous ranking of industries by concentration.

🔹Unambiguous = clear, no confusion.
🔹If Index says Industry X is more concentrated than Y, that ranking should not flip.

The concentration measure should be a function of the combined market share of firms rather than the absolute size of the market or industry.

🔹Use percentages, not dollars.

If the number of firms increases, then concentration should decrease. However, if the new entrant is large enough, then concentration may go up.

🔹Normally, more firms = lower concentration.
🔹Exception: If a giant firm enters, it may raise concentration.
📌 Merger Effect: Before Merger: Firm A = 20%, Firm B = 15%
After Merger: New Firm = 35% → Market concentration increases.
📌 Key Exam Point: Concentration increases when large firms gain market share or when firms merge, and usually decreases when more firms enter the market.
Chapter 3 | Slide 7
Concentration Curve

The Concentration Curve and Measures of Market Concentration – It plot the cumulative market share from the largest to the smallest.

🔹X-axis: firms ranked from biggest to smallest (1st firm, 2nd firm, 3rd firm...).
🔹Y-axis: running total of market share percent (e.g., after 3 firms, you have 60% of the market).

It may provide information on the structural characteristics of the market.

🔹The shape of the curve tells you how skewed the market is.
🔹Very steep start → high concentration (few firms dominate).
🔹Gentle slope → low concentration (many firms share the market).

Market A is the highly concentrated industry. A single firm has 100% market share.

🔹Industry A: one firm sells everything → highest concentration possible.

For instance, for the first five largest firms, market C is more concentrated than B. But, for the number of firms greater than five, market B is more concentrated than C.

🔹This shows a problem with fixed-top firm ratios (like CR5).
🔹Different concentration indices may rank markets differently depending on how many firms they include.
📊 Concentration Curve: Cumulative Market Share vs Cumulative Number of Firms
📌 Key Exam Point: A concentration curve plots cumulative firms against cumulative market share and helps analyze market structure. A monopoly market has the highest concentration.
Chapter 3 | Slide 8
Concentration Ratio (CR)

Concentration Ratio (Cr) is the most popular and simplest index for measurement of market concentration or monopoly power.

🔹This is the index that people use most often because it's easy to calculate and understand.
🔹"Simplest" means you just add up the market shares of the biggest firms – no complicated math.

It is defined as the share of market held by r largest firms, where r is an arbitrary number.

🔹"r" is just a placeholder – you choose how many top firms to include.
🔹Example: If r = 4, you add the market shares of the 4 largest firms. That sum is CR4.

The normal practice is to take r = 4.

🔹In most real-world studies, people use the top 4 firms.
🔹CR4 is so common that many people just say "concentration ratio" and mean CR4.
CR₄ = S₁ + S₂ + S₃ + S₄ | Example: 30% + 25% + 20% + 15% = 90%
⚠️ Classic Problem with CR:
Industry A: 97% + 1% + 1% + 1% = 100% CR₄ (Near Monopoly)
Industry B: 25% + 25% + 25% + 25% = 100% CR₄ (Equal Oligopoly)
Same CR₄ but completely different market structures!
📌 Key Exam Point: Concentration Ratio (CR) measures the combined market share of the largest firms, but cannot show how market shares are distributed among them.
Chapter 3 | Slide 9
Limitations of the Concentration Ratio (CR)

Limitation of the concentration ratio – This index has two limitations: The arbitrary selection of r and The concentration ratio indicates only a single point on the concentration curve without information on the size distribution of the r firms.

🔹First limitation: Picking r = 4 or r = 5 is somewhat random – a different r can give a different ranking of industries.
🔹Second limitation: CR only tells you the total share of the top r firms, not how that share is split among them.
🔹Example: CR4 = 80% could mean one firm has 77% and three have 1% each – or all four have 20% each. CR alone cannot tell the difference.

The Arbitrary Selection of r – when the concentration curves intersect, the measure (Cr) will give different ranking of industries for different values of r.

🔹At CR3, Market C may be more concentrated. At CR6, Market B may be more concentrated.
🔹This is exactly what happens when concentration curves cross – CR gives inconsistent rankings.
📌 Key Exam Point: The main limitations of CR are the arbitrary selection of r and its failure to show the size distribution among the largest firms.
Chapter 3 | Slide 10
Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)

In general, the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index measures competition in a particular industry by examining the proportion of all existing players' market share.

🔹HHI looks at every firm in the market, not just the top few.
🔹Formula: HHI = s1² + s2² + s3² + ... + sn² (where s = market share as a percent).

Advantages: The index reflects the distribution of market share and gives weight to the largest players proportionately. So, it's a better measure than the concentration ratio.

🔹Because shares are squared, a large firm gets much more weight than a small one.
🔹A firm with 50% share contributes 2500 to HHI, while a 1% firm contributes only 1.
🔹This solves the CR problem: HHI distinguishes between one giant + many smalls vs. several equal-sized firms.
HHI = S₁² + S₂² + S₃² + ... + Sₙ²
📊 HHI Examples:
Monopoly (100%): HHI = 10,000
Equal Duopoly (50%+50%): HHI = 5,000
Unequal Duopoly (75%+25%): HHI = 6,250
100 equal firms (1% each): HHI = 100
📌 Key Exam Point: HHI is calculated by summing the squared market shares of all firms and is considered a more comprehensive measure of concentration than CR.
Chapter 3 | Slide 11
Interpretation of HHI

The HHI gives added weight to the biggest industries. The higher the index, the more the market concentration and the less open market competition.

🔹Higher HHI = Higher concentration = Less competition.
🔹More unequal market shares = Higher HHI.

A monopoly would have HHI of 10,000. An industry with 100 competitors each having 1% market share would have HHI of 100.

🔹Monopoly: one firm 100% → 100² = 10,000.
🔹100 equal firms: each 1% → 100 × (1²) = 100.

If two firms have market share of 50% each, HHI = 5000. If two firms have market share of 75% & 25%, HHI = 6250. Thus, as the inequality increases, the HHI index becomes greater.

🔹50² + 50² = 2500 + 2500 = 5000.
🔹75² + 25² = 5625 + 625 = 6250.
📌 Key Exam Point: The higher the HHI, the greater the market concentration and the lower the level of competition.
Chapter 3 | Slide 12
HHI and Merger Decisions

In recognition of its advantage, the HHI has been accepted as a measure of concentration in the United States of America, especially as a guideline to decide merger cases.

🔹US antitrust authorities use HHI to evaluate mergers.

Un-concentrated markets are defined as those in which HHI is 1000 or less.

🔹HHI ≤ 1000 = Unconcentrated market.
🔹1000 < HHI < 2500 = Moderately concentrated.
🔹HHI ≥ 2500 = Highly concentrated.

Merger activities increase HHI. When two firms with market share of S1 and S2 merged, the HHI increases by 2S1S2.

🔹Formula: ΔHHI = 2 × S₁ × S₂.
ΔHHI = 2 × S₁ × S₂
📌 Example: Firm A = 20%, Firm B = 10%
After merger: ΔHHI = 2 × 20 × 10 = 400 points increase
📌 Key Exam Point: HHI is an important tool for evaluating mergers. A merger increases HHI by 2S₁S₂, and authorities may block mergers that significantly increase concentration.
Chapter 3 | Slide 13
Advantages and Disadvantages of HHI

Advantages: The index reflects the distribution of market share and gives weight to the largest players proportionately. So, it's a better measure than the concentration ratio.

🔹✅ HHI considers all firms in the market.
🔹✅ Gives more importance to large firms by squaring market shares.
🔹✅ Provides a more accurate picture than CR.

Disadvantages: The index doesn't take into account the possibility of entering new companies. Market power is less relevant when barriers to entry are low. It is challenging to collect market share data for the entire company. Another problem is when the supply comes from imports, it may be challenging to track down each foreign company.

🔹❌ Ignores potential entrants (HHI is a snapshot of current market).
🔹❌ Difficult data collection – need every firm's market share.
🔹❌ Import problem – foreign suppliers hard to track.
📌 Key Exam Point: HHI is superior to CR because it uses all firms' market shares, but it may be difficult to calculate and ignores potential entrants.
Chapter 3 | Slide 14
Additional Limitations of HHI

Sensitive to market definitions. If you calculate market share for the entire food industry, the market may appear less concentrated. However, if you focus on a subsegment, such as the bakery market, you may reach a different conclusion.

🔹How you define the market changes the HHI dramatically.
🔹A company may have 2% of total food market but 60% of frozen pizza subsegment – very different HHI values.

It says nothing about differentiation. Revenue is sensitive to pricing strategy. Differentiation allows firms to gain market power and charge higher prices.

🔹HHI only looks at market shares, not product quality or brand image.
🔹A differentiated product can charge high prices – high revenue share even with low volume share.
📌 Key Exam Point: HHI is sensitive to market definition and does not account for product differentiation or pricing strategies.
Chapter 3 | Slide 15
Entropy Index (EI)

For a monopoly firm (n=1) the Entropy coefficient takes the value of zero which means no uncertainty and there is maximum concentration.

🔹Monopoly: EI = 0 → Maximum concentration, no uncertainty.

Thus, we find inverse relationship between the Entropy coefficient (EI) and the degree of market concentration.

🔹High concentration → Low EI.
🔹Low concentration (many equal firms) → High EI.

If there are n firms, all equal in size, then: EI = log n

🔹10 equal firms → EI = log(10) = 1.
🔹100 equal firms → EI = log(100) = 2.

EI is similar to HHI, but the difference is that EI gives relatively more weight to the smaller firms.

🔹HHI squares shares → big firms get heavy weight.
🔹EI uses logarithms → small firms contribute more proportionally.
EI = Σ Sᵢ × log(1/Sᵢ) | For n equal firms: EI = log n
📌 Key Exam Point: Entropy Index has an inverse relationship with market concentration: higher EI means lower concentration and greater competition.
Chapter 3 | Slide 16
Gini Coefficient (GC) and Lorenz Curve

Gini coefficient (GC) is an index used to measure equality/inequality.

🔹GC ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality).
🔹GC = 0 means all firms have identical market shares. GC close to 1 means one firm has almost everything.

It is used to plot cumulative market share against the cumulative percentage of firms. In this case, firms are cumulated from the smallest to the largest unlike that of the concentration curve.

🔹X-axis: cumulative % of firms (from smallest to largest).
🔹Y-axis: cumulative % of market share owned by those firms.

Limitation of Gini coefficient: it favors equality of market shares without regard to the number of equal sized firms. GC equal to zero for two firms with 50% market share each or 3 firms with 33.3% market share each. Thus, GC=0 implies equal level of competition, but market structures are very different.

🔹GC cannot distinguish between 2 equal firms and 100 equal firms – both give GC = 0.
🔹But a market with 2 equal firms (duopoly) is much more concentrated than a market with 100 equal firms.
GC = Area between Lorenz curve and diagonal / Total area under diagonal | 0 ≤ GC ≤ 1
📌 Key Exam Point: GC measures inequality in market shares but cannot distinguish between different numbers of equal-sized firms.
Chapter 3 | Slide 17
Lerner Index (Monopoly Power)

By substitution, we have: L = (P - MC) / P

🔹This is the Lerner Index – a measure of monopoly power for a single firm.
🔹P = price the firm charges. MC = marginal cost of production.
🔹If P = MC, L = 0 → perfect competition (no monopoly power).
🔹If P >> MC, L is close to 1 → high monopoly power.

Where e is the price elasticity of demand and hence, LI is the inverse of e, which is price elasticity of demand. Thus, L = 1 / |e|

🔹If demand is very elastic (e = -10), L = 0.1 → low monopoly power.
🔹If demand is inelastic (e = -1.1), L ≈ 0.91 → high monopoly power.
Lerner Index = (P - MC) / P = 1 / |e|
📌 Key Insight: Market power is limited by how easily customers can switch away when prices rise (price elasticity of demand).
Chapter 3 | Slide 18
Concentration and Profit

Concentration and profit: A firm derives market power or monopoly power in the situation of concentration.

🔹Concentration creates an environment where firms can exercise market power.
🔹Market power = ability to raise prices above competitive levels without losing all customers.

Bain was the first to make empirical study on the relationship between concentration & profit. He argued that there is strong linkage between the two and hence profit rate can be a measure of concentration.

🔹Joe Bain (1950s) – pioneer in industrial organization economics.
🔹He found positive correlation: more concentration → higher profits.
🔬 Researchable Area ? ? ? ? ?
Possible research topics: Which concentration index best predicts profit? Does the relationship hold across different countries or time periods? How do barriers to entry change the concentration-profit link?
📌 Key Exam Point: Theory says concentration → market power → higher profits, but precise estimation is still debated.
Chapter 3 | Slide 19
Determinants of Concentration: The Deterministic Approach

Determinants of Concentration – Why concentration is high in some industries but not in others?

🔹Some industries (like commercial aircraft) have 2–3 firms. Others (like restaurants) have thousands. Why?
🔹Answer lies in technology, costs, market size, and history.

The Deterministic Approach (Scale-Economies) – Concentrated market structure could come from persistent scale economies.

🔹Scale economies = producing more units lowers the average cost per unit.
🔹If large firms have much lower costs than small firms, the market will naturally become concentrated.
🔹Example: A car factory costs $1 billion. Make 1 million cars → cost per car = $1,000. Make only 1,000 cars → cost per car = $1 million – impossible to compete.
📊 LAC Shapes & Market Structure:
Constant Cost (Flat) → Indeterminate → Varies
Rapid Fall (Steep down) → Natural Monopoly → Very High Concentration
Rapid Rise (Steep up) → Perfect Competition → Very Low Concentration
Constant Range (U-shaped) → Mixed/Oligopoly → Moderate Concentration
📌 Key Exam Point: The shape of the Long-Run Average Cost (LAC) curve determines market structure and concentration levels.
Chapter 3 | Slide 20
LAC Curve Shapes: Summary

Constant Cost Case – In this case no cost advantages or disadvantages are associated with large-scale production. Market structure is indeterminate.

🔹Constant cost = average cost is the same whether you produce 1 unit or 1 million units.
🔹Example: Simple handcrafts, some services, basic agriculture.

Rapid fall in Unit Production Costs – This is the case of natural monopoly. A single firm can supply the entire market demand at lower cost than multiple firms.

🔹Natural monopoly examples: Water utilities, electricity grids, railroads.

Rapid Rise in Unit Production Costs – Diseconomies at small scale. Market structure is competitive with many small firms.

🔹Examples: Hair salons, local plumbing, small-scale farming.

Constant Range of Minimum LAC Curve – Both large and small firms can co-exist. Market concentration is moderate.

🔹Examples: Automobile manufacturing, publishing, garment manufacturing.

Chapter 4 | Slide 1
Introduction and Brief Review of Determinants of Market Structure

This chapter focuses on market power – the ability of a firm to raise prices above competitive levels.

🔹"Measurement" means how we quantify market power (e.g., Lerner Index, price-cost margins).
🔹"Determinant" means what causes market power (e.g., entry barriers, product differentiation).
🔹"Prone" likely means vulnerability or tendency toward market power.
🔹"Cons" means disadvantages or negative consequences of market power for society.

Definition of market structure – It is the pattern or form or manner in which the different constituent parts (i.e. sellers and buyers) of that body are arranged/linked together.

🔹"Pattern or form or manner" means the way a market is organized – like a blueprint.
🔹Constituent parts = the players in the market: sellers (firms) and buyers (consumers).

It is the characteristics of the organisation and composition of a market that exercises a strategic influence on the nature of competition and pricing within the market.

🔹Characteristics = features like number of firms, product differences, entry barriers.
🔹Organisation and composition = how the market is structured (e.g., one seller vs. many).
🔹Strategic influence = these features actively shape how firms compete and set prices.
Chapter 4 | Slide 2
Determinants of Market Structure (Overview)

Determinants of market structure – Market Concentration: number of buyers and sellers of a specific market/industry and size/power/structure. (As discussed in earlier chapters)

🔹Number of buyers and sellers: few sellers → oligopoly; many sellers → competition.
🔹Size/power/structure: not just count, but how big each firm is and how much power they have.

The degree of product differentiation: It indicates the extent to which a seller has some degree of independence (i.e. some market power) in pricing and other marketing decisions.

🔹Product differentiation = making your product seem different from competitors.
🔹More differentiation → more independence in pricing (customers see fewer substitutes).

The Condition of Barriers to Entry & Exit to/from the Market

🔹Barriers to entry = obstacles that make it hard for new firms to start competing.
🔹High entry barriers protect existing firms and keep concentration high.

The degree of vertical integration with suppliers

🔹Vertical integration = a firm owns its suppliers (backward) or its distributors (forward).
🔹More vertical integration can increase market power by controlling key inputs.
📊 Summary of Determinants: 1. Market Concentration | 2. Product Differentiation | 3. Entry/Exit Barriers | 4. Vertical Integration | 5. Economies of Scale
Chapter 4 | Slide 3
Entry Barriers (Introduction)

Entry Barriers – An entry condition shows the relative ease with which new firms can join the category of sellers in the market.

🔹Entry condition = how hard or easy it is to become a new competitor.

Without entry barriers then even the most complete monopoly is open to competition from new entrants.

🔹Even a 100% monopoly would not last long if anyone can enter freely.
🔹A single coffee shop in a town with no barriers – the next day, ten more coffee shops open.

Concentration must be considered in relationship to level of barriers to entry and exit.

🔹Concentration alone does not tell the whole story.
🔹A market with 2 firms but zero entry barriers may behave competitively (threat of entry).
🔹A market with 10 firms but very high entry barriers may behave like a monopoly.
📌 Key Insight: Concentration + Entry Barriers = True Market Power. Threat of entry can keep even concentrated markets competitive.
Chapter 4 | Slide 4
Types of Barriers to Entry

Types of entry Barriers may include: Government policies; Economics of Scale & Natural Monopolies; Large Initial Capital Requirement; Market/business strategies of firms with the aim of deterring new entry.

🔹Government policies: licenses, patents, regulations, tariffs.
🔹Economies of scale & natural monopolies: large firms have cost advantages.
🔹Large initial capital requirement: need huge money to start (e.g., semiconductor factory).
🔹Business strategies: predatory pricing, product proliferation, advertising blitzes.
📊 Four Main Types of Entry Barriers:
1. Government Policies (licenses, patents, regulations)
2. Economies of Scale & Natural Monopolies
3. Large Initial Capital Requirements
4. Business Strategies (predatory pricing, product proliferation)
📌 Key Exam Point: Entry barriers can be natural (scale economies) or artificial (government policies, business strategies).
Chapter 4 | Slide 5
Government Policies & Practices as Barriers

Government Policies & Practices – Monopolies are often established by governments. Governments grant monopolies or exclusive franchise for electric, telecommunication, water and other utility services.

🔹Exclusive franchise = only one company is allowed to operate in a region.
🔹Why? Duplicating utility infrastructure would be wasteful – natural monopoly justification.

Governments grant patents: Meant to encourage inventions & Innovations by protecting innovators & inventors. Governments grant inventors exclusive right to use their discoveries for certain period of time (e.g. 17 years for patents). Patents increase market concentration & reduce competition.

🔹Patents = a temporary legal monopoly (usually 20 years).
🔹Purpose: Reward innovation by letting inventors charge high prices without competition.
🔹After patent expires, competition returns (e.g., generic drugs).

Government regulations in the form of licensing requirements restrict entry. E.g. Licensing requirements for lawyers, accountants, medical doctors, etc. are aimed at protecting consumers against incompetent practitioners.

🔹Licensing = you need government permission to do business.
🔹Justification: consumer protection (quality, safety, competence).
🔹Effect: reduces competition, raises prices, benefits existing professionals.
📌 Government-Created Barriers: Exclusive franchises, Patents, Licensing requirements – these create concentration by law, not by market forces.
Chapter 4 | Slide 6
Business Strategies Aimed at Deterring Entry

Pricing strategies: predatory pricing and limit pricing

🔹Predatory pricing = temporarily lowering prices below cost to drive out a new competitor, then raising prices later.
🔹Limit pricing = setting prices just low enough to make entry unprofitable.

Single Ownership of an Essential Input

🔹If one firm owns the only source of a key raw material, no new firm can enter without paying that firm.
🔹Example: De Beers historically owned most diamond mines.

Product/Brand Proliferation/Differentiation

🔹Product proliferation = flooding the market with many varieties – shelf space is full.
🔹Brand differentiation = making consumers loyal through advertising and quality.

Excess Capacity – Note that this determinant of entry barrier is an element of business strategy/conduct.

🔹Excess capacity = having factories that are not fully used.
🔹Threat: If a new firm enters, incumbent can instantly increase production and flood the market.
📊 Business Strategy Barriers: Predatory/Limit Pricing, Control of essential inputs, R&D investment, Product proliferation, Strategic location, Heavy advertising, Excess capacity.
Chapter 4 | Slide 7
Economies of Scale (Detailed)

Cost of production and scale of operation (economies of scale) as its determinant: Scale determines costs & costs determine prices, & prices determine market share, and all these together determine the profitability of the firm.

🔹Scale → determines costs per unit.
🔹Costs → determine prices (firms set price above cost).
🔹Prices → determine market share (lower price wins more customers).
🔹All together → determine profitability.

Horizontal LAC: Indeterminate market structure – There is no cost advantage (or disadvantage) associated with any scale of operation.

🔹Horizontal LAC = flat long-run average cost curve.
🔹No cost advantage = small and large firms have the same cost per unit.

Diversification (Introduction)

The typical unit of analysis in microeconomic theory is a single-product, single-plant firm serving a single market.

🔹In basic economics textbooks, we assume a firm makes just one thing in one factory and sells it in one market.
🔹This simplification helps build theories, but real life is more complex.
🔹Examples: A wheat farm (single product, single market) fits this model.
🔹But most real firms are not this simple – which is why we study diversification.

In practice, however, many firms produce range of products and serve on a number of markets.

🔹Real companies usually sell many different products and operate in multiple markets.
🔹Example: Samsung makes phones, TVs, washing machines, semiconductors – and sells worldwide.
🔹This is the reality that diversification theory tries to explain.
🔹So microeconomic theory's simple model is a starting point, not the full picture.

Thus, such companies are described as diversified.

🔹Diversified = not putting all eggs in one basket.
🔹A diversified firm has multiple products, multiple markets, or both.
🔹Diversification is the opposite of specialization (single-product focus).
🔹Most large corporations today are diversified to some degree.

Diversification occurs when a single-product firm changes itself into a multi-product/market

🔹A firm that used to make only one product decides to start making two or more products.
🔹Or it takes its existing product and sells it in new geographic markets.
🔹The change can be gradual (internal growth) or sudden (acquisition).
🔹This transformation is a major strategic decision.

In diversification firms involve themselves in products related to their initial activity, providing a degree of coherence and economic logic that may have initially.

🔹Often, the new products are related to the original business – same technology, same customers, same distribution.
🔹Coherence = there is a logical connection between old and new activities (not random).
🔹Economic logic = the firm can share resources, skills, or brand names.
🔹Example: A tractor company starts making lawn mowers – related by engine technology and dealer network.

However, where the firm diversifies into products that are unrelated, the economic benefits and logic are not so easily identified.

🔹Unrelated diversification = a computer company buying a hotel chain.
🔹The benefits are harder to see because there is no obvious sharing of technology, customers, or skills.
🔹The economic logic might be financial (spreading risk) or managerial (better management).
🔹But it's less obvious and more controversial than related diversification.
📌 Key Takeaway: Diversification is the expansion of a firm into new products or markets. Related diversification leverages existing capabilities; unrelated diversification is riskier but can spread financial risk.

Types of Diversification Strategies

The strategies of diversification can include: Internal development of new products or markets, Acquisition of a firm, Alliance with a complementary company, Licensing of new technologies, and Distributing or importing a products line manufactured by another firm.

🔹Internal development = creating new products from scratch using your own R&D and factories.
🔹Acquisition = buying another company that already makes the product you want.
🔹Alliance = partnering with another firm (joint venture, strategic partnership) without full ownership.
🔹Licensing = paying another company for the right to use their technology or brand.
🔹Distributing/importing = selling products made by someone else under your name or alongside yours.
🔹These are different paths to the same destination: becoming a multi-product firm.

Generally, the final strategy involves a combination of these options.

🔹Most real-world diversification uses more than one method at the same time.
🔹Example: A company might internally develop some new products, acquire a competitor for others, and license technology from a third party.
🔹"Combination" means flexibility – no single best way works for all situations.

There are three types of diversification: Concentric diversification, Horizontal diversification, Conglomerate diversification

🔹Concentric (related) – new products related to old ones.
🔹Horizontal – new products aimed at same customers, but not necessarily related in technology.
🔹Conglomerate (unrelated) – completely different products, different customers, different industries.
🔹These three categories are the standard classification in business strategy.
📊 Three Types of Diversification:
🔹 Concentric (Related) – Same technology/customers (e.g., smartphone → smartwatch)
🔹 Horizontal – Same customers, different products (e.g., coffee shop adding pastries)
🔹 Conglomerate (Unrelated) – Different customers, different products (e.g., tobacco company buying hotel chain)

Concentric Diversification (Definition)

Concentric diversification; a company seeks to grow and develop by adding new products to its existing product lines to attract new customers.

🔹The goal is growth by expanding the product portfolio.
🔹"Attract new customers" – not just selling more to existing customers, but reaching people who didn't buy before.
🔹Example: A toothpaste brand adds mouthwash – some toothpaste buyers also buy mouthwash, but mouthwash also attracts people who don't use that toothpaste.
🔹The new product is related, but the customer base expands.

It is also called convergent diversification.

🔹Convergent = coming together, converging toward a common core.
🔹Another name for the same concept – used interchangeably in some textbooks.
🔹"Convergent" emphasizes the similarity and connection between old and new.

A growth strategy that involves adding new products or services that are related to the company's existing business.

🔹This is the core definition – relatedness is the key.
🔹Relatedness can be in technology, distribution, customers, or brand.
🔹Example: A smartphone maker adds smartwatches – related by technology and customer base.
🔹The opposite would be a smartphone maker starting a restaurant chain (unrelated).

Under concentric diversification, a firm adds new but related products.

🔹New products are different from old ones (otherwise it's just line extension, not diversification).
🔹But they share some common thread with the existing business.
🔹The similarity is what makes concentric diversification less risky than unrelated diversification.
🔹Example: A bicycle company starts making electric scooters – different but related (two-wheeled personal transport).
📌 Key Concept: Concentric (related) diversification leverages existing competencies, reduces risk, and creates economies of scope. The new product shares technology, customers, or distribution with existing products.

Concentric Diversification (Examples)

Examples: A company that manufactures industrial cements might decide to diversify into cements to be sold via retailers.

🔹Same product (cement), but different customer type and distribution channel.
🔹Industrial cement → sold in bulk to construction companies.
🔹Retail cement → sold in bags to homeowners or small contractors via hardware stores.
🔹Technology is identical; marketing and packaging change – classic concentric diversification.

The technology would be the same but the marketing effort would need to change.

🔹Same manufacturing process, same chemistry.
🔹New marketing: different packaging (small bags), different advertising (DIY customers), different distribution (retail stores).
🔹This shows that concentric diversification does not mean zero change – just less change than unrelated diversification.
🔹The firm leverages its production expertise while learning new marketing skills.

An orange juice brand releases a new "smooth" orange juice drink alongside its hero product, the orange juice "with bits"

🔹Hero product = the main, best-selling product (with bits).
🔹New product = smooth (no bits) – still orange juice, still same brand.
🔹Appeals to customers who don't like bits – expands the market within the same category.
🔹Technology (squeezing oranges) is identical; recipe slightly changed.

An automobile company adds a solar-powered car to its eco-friendly auto line.

🔹Eco-friendly auto line already exists (hybrids, electrics).
🔹Solar-powered car is a new technology but still related to eco-friendly vehicles.
🔹Same customers (environmentally conscious buyers), same distribution (car dealerships).
🔹Technology is different (solar panels vs. batteries), but the strategic fit is clear.

A manufacturer of computers who is experiencing a decline in PC sales. Thus, to boost revenue, the computer maker can start selling laptops and tablets.

🔹Declining PC sales → need new products to maintain revenue and profit.
🔹Laptops and tablets are related to desktop PCs – same customers, same distribution, similar technology.
🔹Revenue boost comes from capturing customers who are shifting away from desktops.
🔹Without this move, the company would shrink with the desktop market.

A pizza restaurant adds pasta and calzones to its menu.

🔹Same restaurant, same kitchen, same customers, same delivery drivers.
🔹Pasta and calzones are related to pizza (Italian cuisine, dough-based, baked).
🔹Low-risk diversification – leverages existing assets (oven, staff, location).
🔹This is a small-scale example of concentric diversification in a small business.
📌 Key Insight: Concentric diversification is lower-risk because it builds on existing capabilities, technology, and customer relationships.

Horizontal Diversification (Definition)

Horizontal diversification: it is a growth strategy in which a company seeks to add new products to its existing lines that will appeal to its existing customers.

🔹The key phrase: existing customers (not new customers, unlike concentric).
🔹New products are added to existing product lines – not creating entirely new lines.
🔹The goal is to sell more to the people who already buy from you.
🔹Example: A coffee shop adds pastries – same customers (coffee drinkers) now buy more.

Horizontal Diversification is a growth strategy in which a company seeks to add to its existing product lines with improved versions of the originals or with new products that add value and appeal for its current customers.

🔹Two ways to do it: Improved versions (better quality, new features) or new products (different but still appealing to same customers).
🔹"Add value and appeal" means making customers happier or giving them more reasons to stay loyal.
🔹The customer base does not expand – the share of wallet expands.

The Company adds new products or services that are technologically or commercially unrelated (but not always) to current products, but which may appeal to current customers.

🔹Here is the key difference from concentric: unrelated is possible in horizontal diversification.
🔹Example: A gas station adds a convenience store – selling snacks and drinks is commercially unrelated to selling gasoline, but same customers (drivers) buy both.
🔹"Not always" means sometimes the products are related, sometimes not – the defining feature is the same customers, not same technology.

When a company decides to use horizontal diversification, they might add products to one of their current product lines that do not relate to the other products in the line.

🔹Within a single product line, they might add something unrelated to the other products.
🔹Example: A coffee shop (hot drinks line) starts selling cold bottled water – not related to coffee, but same customers.
🔹This is different from adding a whole new product line – it's adding to an existing line.

This can allow for new products to appeal to customers that already make purchases at a business by offering new ways to meet their needs.

🔹"New ways to meet their needs" – customers have multiple needs; the firm is trying to satisfy more of them.
🔹Example: A bookstore adds a cafe – the same customer now spends more time and money at the same store.
🔹This increases customer loyalty and lifetime value.
📌 Key Concept: Horizontal diversification targets existing customers with new products. The goal is to increase share of wallet and deepen customer relationships.

Horizontal Diversification (Process and Benefits)

What steps should be followed? Horizontal diversification works by identifying a need for a product and trying to fill that need with a new product that does not already exist in a company's product lines.

🔹Step 1: Identify a customer need that is not currently being met by your products.
🔹Step 2: Create or acquire a product that fills that need.
🔹The new product must be new to the company (not just a variation of what you already sell).
🔹The need exists among your existing customers – that's what makes it horizontal.

To determine where they might need to engage in horizontal diversification, a company might conduct market research by offering feedback surveys to its customers, looking into consumer behaviour or analysing sales performance for different products that they offer.

🔹Surveys – ask customers directly: "What else would you buy from us?"
🔹Consumer behavior – study what customers buy from competitors that you could also offer.
🔹Sales performance – see which products sell well together (market basket analysis).
🔹Market research reduces the risk of diversification by providing data, not just intuition.

This form of diversification is desirable if the present customers are loyal to the current products and if the new products have a good quality and are well promoted and priced.

🔹Customer loyalty – if customers trust your brand, they are more likely to try your new products.
🔹Good quality – new products must meet or exceed expectations, or loyalty will be damaged.
🔹Well promoted – customers need to know the new product exists (advertising, in-store displays).
🔹Well priced – price must be competitive and appropriate for the value offered.

Examples: Local retail having a reputation for selling quality jewelry can diversified using horizontal diversification, by start selling premium perfumes to magnify the revenues.

🔹Same customers (jewelry buyers) are often also perfume buyers (luxury goods).
🔹The reputation for quality in one luxury category extends to another.
🔹Revenue magnification = selling more to the same customers without acquiring new ones.

A company was making note books earlier now they are also entering into pen market through its new product.

🔹Notebooks and pens are complementary products – often bought together.
🔹Same customers (students, office workers).
🔹The company can cross-sell: buy a notebook, get a pen.
🔹This deepens the relationship with existing customers and increases average transaction value.

One clear benefit of horizontal diversification is the chance for a company to grow its product lines.

🔹Product line growth means offering more choices to the same customer base.
🔹More choices → higher likelihood that customers find exactly what they want → more sales.
🔹Growing product lines also signals a healthy, expanding company to investors.
🔹But too many products can confuse customers – balance is important.
📌 Key Benefits: Increases share of wallet, deepens customer loyalty, leverages existing brand trust, and grows product lines without acquiring new customers.

Conglomerate Diversification (Definition)

Conglomerate diversification (lateral/unrelated diversification): a company seeks to develop or grows by adding totally unrelated products and markets to its existing business.

🔹Totally unrelated – no technological, commercial, or customer overlap.
🔹Example: A tobacco company buys a hotel chain – no connection.
🔹The new products are for new customers in new markets.
🔹This is the most extreme form of diversification and the riskiest.

Mainly involves offering new products or services to new customers and used as a strategy to grow on the market and gain new customers, which are no interested in current offerings.

🔹New customers – because existing customers are not interested in the new product.
🔹The goal is growth by entering entirely new customer segments.
🔹The current offerings are irrelevant to these new customers – they wouldn't buy your old products.
🔹Example: A defense contractor starts making baby food – new product, new customers, no connection.

New products and services created during conglomerate diversification strategy, are usually totally different than products or services currently in the offering.

🔹"Totally different" means no shared technology, no shared distribution, no shared brand.
🔹The company is essentially starting a new business from scratch (or by acquisition).
🔹Example: A car manufacturer starts a bank – cars and banking have nothing in common.
🔹This is why conglomerate diversification is often called "unrelated diversification."

Main goals of conglomerate diversification is to grow sales and profits in the same time maintaining current marketing activities.

🔹Dual goal – grow overall company sales and profits.
🔹Maintain current marketing – the old business continues unchanged; new business is separate.
🔹The company becomes a portfolio of unrelated businesses.
🔹Example: Berkshire Hathaway owns insurance, railroads, utilities, candy, furniture – each runs independently.

It has very little relationship with the firm's current business.

🔹"Very little" – could be zero relationship, or a tiny, accidental connection.
🔹Example: A newspaper company buys a ski resort – both involve "vacation" vaguely, but no real operational link.
🔹The less relationship, the more "pure" the conglomerate diversification.
🔹Many conglomerates have no relationship at all between their divisions.
📌 Key Concept: Conglomerate (unrelated) diversification is the riskiest form of diversification, but can provide financial synergies and risk spreading across unrelated industries.

Why this types diversification is needed? The main reasons of adopting such a strategy are; First, to improve the profitability and the flexibility of the company, and Second, to get a better acceptance or recognition in capital markets as the company gets bigger.

🔹Reason 1 – Profitability and flexibility – different businesses may have different profit cycles; when one is down, another may be up, stabilizing total profits.
🔹Flexibility – the company can shift resources from slow-growing to fast-growing divisions.
🔹Reason 2 – Capital market recognition – larger companies often get better credit ratings, lower borrowing costs, and more analyst coverage.
🔹Bigger companies may also be seen as "too big to fail" or more prestigious.

Examples – There is no limited example in this case: Conglomerate diversification involves adding new products or services that are significantly unrelated and with no technological or commercial similarities.

🔹"No limited example" – the possibilities are endless; any unrelated combination qualifies.
🔹Significantly unrelated = no meaningful connection in operations, customers, or technology.
🔹Example: A fishing company starts a software firm – completely unrelated.
🔹The more unrelated, the more it fits the conglomerate definition.

Real-life example of Amazon's diversification strategy. Amazon is a multinational company that provides various online services such as; e-commerce, cloud computing, email delivery, online video, music streaming, e-payment, and affiliate marketing.

🔹Then diversified into e-commerce for everything (related by online retail).
🔹Then cloud computing (AWS) – this is conglomerate (unrelated to retail, but hugely profitable).
🔹Then video streaming, music, payments, etc. – some related, some less related.

Apart from this, Amazon also introduced a virtual assistant. Further, it operates brick-and-mortar stores in the United States.

🔹Brick-and-mortar = physical retail stores (Amazon Go, Whole Foods) – related diversification.

Motives for Diversification (Introduction)

Motives for Diversification – Why firms need to engaged with diversification?

🔹This section answers the question: What pushes or pulls a firm to diversify?
🔹Motives can be internal (underused resources) or external (market opportunities).
🔹Understanding motives helps predict which firms will diversify and how.

When a company's current goals, like profit and growth, can no longer be achieved with its current product, diversification may become necessary.

🔹Current goals not achievable = the firm is hitting a wall with its existing products.
🔹Example: Market is saturated (everyone already has one), or growth has stopped.
🔹Diversification becomes "necessary" – not optional – for survival or continued growth.
🔹This is a push factor – the current situation forces change.

Nevertheless, a variety of push factors resulting from the firm's current situation may be responsible for the adoption of a diversification plan.

🔹Push factors = internal or external pressures that drive the firm away from its current path.
🔹Push factors may include: The limited size of the existing market, underutilized assets, and surplus investment resources.

Pull factors: There may also be a number of pull factors, or incentives, for firms to adopt diversification.

🔹Pull factors = attractive opportunities outside the current business that draw the firm in.
🔹Incentives = rewards, benefits, or positive outcomes that make diversification appealing.
🔹Pull factors are about opportunity, not necessity.
🔹Example: A new technology emerges that your firm could exploit, even though you don't need to.

Managers may also be pulled toward diversification where the potential rewards from investing in new market opportunities promise greater profitability than ploughing them back into existing activities.

🔹Potential rewards – higher profits, faster growth, lower risk.
🔹If new opportunities look more profitable than current ones, managers are pulled toward them.

However, any diversification will have a higher degree of uncertainty attached compared with the more certain but limited returns in existing activity.

🔹Uncertainty – you don't know if the new venture will succeed.
🔹Existing activity has "more certain" returns – you have history and data.
🔹But those returns may be "limited" – not growing, maybe declining.
🔹There is a trade-off: certainty but low returns vs. uncertainty but potentially high returns.

Therefore, diversification may be a high-risk strategy because it involves new products, new markets and the commitment of financial and managerial resources for uncertain returns.

🔹High-risk strategy – not for the faint-hearted.
🔹New products (might fail), new markets (might not accept them), new resources (might be wasted).
🔹Uncertain returns = you might lose money, not make it.
🔹This is the dark side of diversification – the reason many firms stay focused.
📌 Key Insight: Diversification motives include both push factors (limited market, underutilized resources) and pull factors (attractive new opportunities). It's a high-risk, high-reward strategy.

Threats and Risks to Managers

The threats and risks of Managers: The pursuit of diversification may be strengthened by the need to make sufficient profits to keep shareholders happy and to maintain the valuation ratio of the firm.

🔹Shareholder happiness – shareholders want growing profits and rising stock prices.
🔹Valuation ratio – e.g., price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio; a key metric that affects stock price.
🔹If current business can't deliver, managers may feel pressured to diversify to satisfy shareholders.

If this cannot be achieved, then shareholders may prefer to see retained earnings returned in the form of dividends.

🔹Retained earnings = profits kept in the company for reinvestment, not paid out.
🔹If diversification doesn't seem promising, shareholders might say: "Just give us the money as dividends."
🔹This is a threat to managers – less money to invest, less growth, smaller company.

The term of the current management may be threatened by a poor stock market performance, either as a consequence of shareholder dissatisfaction or as a result of outside interests purchasing assets they believe to be undervalued.

🔹Poor stock performance → share price falls.
🔹Shareholder dissatisfaction → they may vote to replace management.
🔹Outside interests (corporate raiders) may buy undervalued shares and take over the company.
🔹Takeover → current managers often lose their jobs.
🔹So managers have a personal incentive to avoid poor performance, even if that means taking diversification risks.

Therefore, managers must also consider the threats and risks posed to the firm as a consequence of diversification.

🔹Diversification can fail – losing money, wasting resources, damaging brand.
🔹It can also distract management from the core business, which then declines.
🔹So managers face a double risk: Risk of not diversifying vs. Risk of diversifying.
🔹Finding the right balance is difficult.

The following can be some of the motives of diversification. Utilization of the firm's resources, Economies of scope and size, Reducing the volatility of profits and risk spreading, Financial synergies, Managerial risks and rewards, The pursuit of growth, Transaction costs, Market power

🔹Utilization of resources – using what you already have more fully.
🔹Economies of scope & size – cost savings from doing multiple things together.
🔹Reducing profit volatility – smoothing out ups and downs.
🔹Financial synergies – better access to capital, lower borrowing costs.
🔹Managerial risks & rewards – managers protecting their jobs and increasing pay.
🔹Pursuit of growth – growing for its own sake.
🔹Transaction costs – cheaper to do internally than to buy from outside.
🔹Market power – becoming strong enough to dominate.
📌 Key Insight: Managers face pressure to diversify from shareholders (push) and have personal incentives (job security, higher pay) to pursue growth through diversification.

Utilization of the Firm's Resources

Utilization of the firm's resources – Making better use of firm's existing assets and competences raises profitability.

🔹Existing assets = factories, equipment, brands, patents, customer relationships – things the firm already owns.
🔹Competences = skills, knowledge, routines, managerial talent – what the firm knows how to do well.
🔹Better use means using them more intensively or in new ways, not letting them sit idle.
🔹Higher utilization → spreads fixed costs over more revenue → raises profitability.

Greater use could be made of indivisible plant and equipment by making new products.

🔹Indivisible = you cannot buy half a factory or half a machine; it comes as a whole unit.
🔹If a factory can produce 1 million units but you only sell 600,000 units, you have spare capacity.
🔹Making new products using that spare capacity raises utilization without buying new equipment.
🔹Example: A car factory that also makes pickup trucks or vans on the same assembly line.

The distribution and logistics system by distributing related goods to the same outlets.

🔹Distribution and logistics = trucks, warehouses, delivery routes, inventory systems.
🔹If you already deliver to 1,000 stores, adding a new product to those same deliveries costs very little extra.
🔹The fixed cost of the distribution network is already paid – spread it over more products.
🔹Example: A soda company starts distributing bottled water using the same trucks and routes.

The marketing department to advertise and promote the new product using its accumulated knowledge and expertise of particular markets and customers.

🔹Marketing department already knows which ads work, which media to use, which customer segments respond.
🔹Using that knowledge for a new related product is cheaper than starting from zero.
🔹Accumulated knowledge = years of learning about customer behavior, pricing, promotion.
🔹Example: A laundry detergent company uses its marketing expertise to launch a fabric softener.

The brand name to sell new products using the goodwill built up for its existing branded products.

🔹Goodwill = customer trust, positive associations, brand loyalty.
🔹A strong brand name can be extended to new products – customers are more likely to try them.
🔹This is called brand extension – leveraging brand equity.
🔹Example: Apple used its brand reputation from computers to sell iPhones, iPads, and AirPods.

Retained earnings that are not required to develop current activities can be used for investment in new activities than keeping them in the non-interest earning form of cash.

🔹Retained earnings = profits kept inside the company instead of paid as dividends.
🔹If current activities don't need all that cash for growth, it sits idle or earns low interest.
🔹Diversification gives that cash a productive use – potentially earning higher returns.
🔹Better than letting cash sit in a bank account earning almost nothing.
📌 Key Insight: Underutilized resources (factories, brands, distribution networks, cash) can be leveraged through diversification to increase profitability without major new investment.

Economies of Scope and Size

Economies of scope and size – Economies of scope arise from the nature of the production function, so that two or more products or activities can be produced more cheaply together than separately.

🔹Economies of scope = cost savings from producing multiple products together.
🔹Cheaper together than separately = the cost of joint production is less than the sum of separate productions.
🔹Example: A bakery makes bread and pastries – same oven, same staff, same store – cheaper than two separate bakeries.

These benefits are not available to single-product firms.

🔹A firm that makes only one product cannot get economies of scope by definition.
🔹Single-product firms focus on economies of scale (making more of the same product cheaper).
🔹To get economies of scope, you must diversify – add at least one more product.
🔹This is a key motive for diversification.

The increase in size of the firm that comes with diversification may also produce economies of size.

🔹Economies of size = cost advantages that come from being a large firm, regardless of product mix.
🔹These are different from economies of scale (which are about volume of a single product).
🔹Larger firms can get bulk discounts, lower borrowing costs, spread fixed costs.
🔹Example: A large diversified firm can negotiate better health insurance rates for all employees.

For example, an increase in size might mean that larger firms may be able to use its buying power to obtain lower cost inputs.

🔹Buying power (monopsony power) – when you buy huge quantities, suppliers give you discounts.
🔹Example: Walmart is so large that it can demand lower prices from suppliers.
🔹This benefit comes from overall size, not from any specific product.
🔹Diversification that increases total purchasing volume can unlock these discounts.

The extent to which this is possible may depend on the degree of relatedness between the various activities of the firm.

🔹If the firm's different divisions buy completely different raw materials, there is no combined buying power.
🔹If they share common inputs (e.g., steel, plastic, packaging), then bulk purchasing works across divisions.
🔹Relatedness matters – unrelated diversification gives less purchasing synergy.

Size may also allow the company to achieve lower management costs through organizational efficiency.

🔹Larger firms can afford specialized managers (e.g., separate CFO, CMO, CTO) – each more efficient.
🔹They can centralize functions (HR, IT, accounting) into shared service centers – lower cost per unit.
🔹Example: One payroll department for 10 divisions is cheaper than 10 separate payroll departments.
📌 Key Insight: Economies of scope (cost savings from joint production) and economies of size (advantages of being large) are powerful motives for diversification.

Reducing Volatility of Profits and Risk Spreading

Reducing the volatility of profits and risk spreading – A single-product, single-market firm is vulnerable to unpredictable and cyclical variations in demand and input costs, as well as to long-term decline in demand.

🔹Volatility = profits go up and down sharply, unpredictably.
🔹Unpredictable variations = sudden changes (e.g., pandemic, new competitor, supply shock).
🔹Cyclical variations = ups and downs with the business cycle (recession vs. boom).
🔹Long-term decline = the product is dying (e.g., landline phones, film cameras).
🔹A single-product firm has no cushion – it rises and falls with that one market.

Diversification is a way for the firm to reduce the dispersion and offset the decline in profits.

🔹Dispersion = how spread out the profit numbers are – high dispersion = high volatility.
🔹Reducing dispersion = making profits more stable year to year.
🔹Offsetting decline = when one product's profits fall, another product's profits rise to compensate.
🔹Diversification acts like a financial portfolio – don't put all eggs in one basket.

Cyclical variations can be offset by the acquisition of products whose sales move counter-cyclically to its existing product, while secular decline can be offset by acquiring products exhibiting long-term growth.

🔹Counter-cyclical = sales go up when the economy goes down, and down when the economy goes up.
🔹Example: Cheap fast food does well in recessions; luxury goods do well in booms – owning both smooths out cycles.
🔹Long-term growth = products with increasing demand over decades (e.g., healthcare, renewable energy).
🔹Acquiring such products offsets a dying core product.

Diversification enables a firm to spread risks by offering a degree of insurance against unexpected changes in any one market for any one product.

🔹Spread risks = don't concentrate all risk in one product or market.
🔹Degree of insurance = not full insurance, but partial protection.
🔹Unexpected changes = new competitor, new regulation, new technology, supply disruption.
🔹If you sell 10 products in 10 markets, one bad event affects only 10% of your business.
📌 Key Insight: Diversification acts as a hedge against market volatility, cyclical downturns, and secular decline, providing more stable and predictable profits.

Financial Synergies

Financial synergies; (Refers to the improvements in financial activities and conditions)

🔹Financial synergies = benefits related to money, capital, and financing – not production or marketing.
🔹Improvements = lower cost of capital, better access to loans, higher credit rating.
🔹Financial activities = raising money (debt, equity), paying dividends, managing cash.

Diversification may limit profit variability and, hence, variations in dividend payments to shareholders; this may give the firm a cost of capital advantage compared with firms whose profits are more variable.

🔹Profit variability = how much profits jump up and down.
🔹Stable profits → stable dividends → shareholders are happier and demand lower returns.
🔹Cost of capital advantage = the firm can borrow at lower interest rates and issue stock at higher prices.
🔹Firms with volatile profits are seen as riskier → lenders charge higher interest → higher cost of capital.

If the firm has a choice between equity and debt finance, then a more stable profit and dividend flow will allow the firm to increase the proportion of its finance raised through debt capital.

🔹Choice between equity and debt = the firm decides how to raise money: sell shares (equity) or borrow (debt).
🔹Stable profits → lenders trust the firm → debt is cheaper and available in larger amounts.
🔹Increase proportion of debt = higher leverage (more borrowed money relative to owner's equity).

Debt capital may also offer tax advantages to the firm, since the interest payments are treated as a cost rather than an element of profit.

🔹Interest payments are deducted from revenue before calculating taxable profit.
🔹This reduces the firm's tax bill – effectively, the government subsidizes part of the interest.
🔹Dividends are not a deductible expense – the company pays tax on the profit first, then pays dividends.
🔹So debt has a tax advantage over equity – one reason firms use debt.
📌 Key Insight: Diversification leads to more stable profits, which lowers the cost of capital and provides tax advantages through increased debt capacity.

Managerial Risks and Rewards

Managerial risks and rewards – Unlike shareholders, senior managers of a corporation are unable to diversify their job risks.

🔹Shareholders can hold stocks in many different companies – if one fails, others may succeed.
🔹Senior managers typically work for one company at a time – their entire career income and reputation depend on that one firm.
🔹They cannot diversify their job risk the way shareholders diversify their investment risk.
🔹This is a key insight: managers are more risk-averse than shareholders.

If the company performs poorly, the managers risk being fired by the shareholders or having the business acquired by another company.

🔹Poor performance = falling profits, falling stock price, losing market share.
🔹Risk of being fired = board of directors (representing shareholders) can replace management.
🔹Risk of acquisition = another company buys the struggling firm – new owners often replace top managers.

As a result, it is in the interests of senior managers to diversify the activities of the firm to reduce the variability of overall profits, dividends and, hence, share price to reduce the risk of their own dismissal.

🔹In their interests = it benefits managers personally.
🔹Diversify activities = move into multiple products/markets to stabilize profits.
🔹Less profit variability → fewer bad years → less chance of being fired.
🔹Stable share price → happy shareholders → board keeps management in place.
🔹So managers have a personal incentive to diversify, even if shareholders might prefer focus.

If managerial rewards are also tied to the size of the firm, then growth by diversification satisfies both their need to protect security of employment and the desire to see the remuneration package increase in size.

🔹Managerial rewards = salary, bonuses, stock options, perks.
🔹Often tied to firm size (revenue, assets, number of employees) – bigger firms pay top managers more.
🔹Diversification → growth in size → higher pay for managers.
🔹Diversification also → stability → job security.
🔹So diversification satisfies both security (avoid firing) and remuneration (higher pay).

However, if managers take diversification too far in pursuit of managerial security, then it may eventually reduce profitability and bring managers into conflict with shareholders.

🔹Too far = excessive, unrelated diversification that destroys value.
🔹Pursuit of managerial security = managers protecting their own jobs at the expense of company performance.
🔹Reduced profitability = diversification fails, costs rise, profits fall.
🔹Conflict with shareholders = shareholders want profit maximization; managers want job security.
🔹This is a classic principal-agent problem.
📌 Key Insight: Managers have personal incentives to diversify (job security, higher pay) that may conflict with shareholder value maximization – a classic principal-agent problem.

The pursuit of growth – Diversifications is thought as a potential component of the company's growth strategy.

🔹Growth is a major corporate goal – not just profit, but getting bigger.
🔹Diversification is one tool among many (market penetration, market development, product development).
🔹Some firms grow organically (same product, more customers); others grow through diversification.

Diversification can help to secure the growth of assets, sales, and earnings in an addition to lowering risks.

🔹Assets = factories, equipment, patents, brand value – diversification can grow these.
🔹Sales = total revenue – new products bring new sales.
🔹Earnings = profits – if new products are profitable.
🔹Lowering risks – diversification spreads risk.
🔹So diversification offers both growth and risk reduction – a powerful combination.

Companies whose main goal is expansion will want to break free from the limitations of their current, slow-growing markets.

🔹Expansion = getting larger, not just more profitable.
🔹Break free = escape the ceiling of a small or stagnant market.
🔹Example: A company selling only in a small country with 1% population growth – it cannot grow fast without leaving that market.

The goal of growth will stimulate the purchase of other companies with portfolios of potentially lucrative new goods if the firm does not already have one.

🔹Purchase of other companies = acquisition (buying another firm).
🔹Portfolios of potentially lucrative new goods = a set of products that could become profitable.
🔹If the firm lacks exciting new products internally, it can buy a company that already has them.

The rate of growth of demand for existing products is a constraint on the growth of the firm. This constraint can be overcome if the firm diversifies into new products that are being sold in faster growing markets.

🔹Constraint = a limit or ceiling.

🔹Diversification breaks that constraint – you enter markets with faster growth.
🔹Example: A typewriter company diversifies into computer printers (growing market).

Diversification is a risky strategy in that all new products do not necessarily succeed in winning profitable positions in markets.

🔹Risky = chance of failure.
🔹Not all new products succeed – many fail.
🔹"Winning profitable positions" = gaining enough market share and pricing power to earn good profits.
🔹The risk is that diversification costs money and may not pay off.
📌 Key Insight: Growth is a powerful motive for diversification, especially when current markets are saturated or slow-growing, but diversification carries inherent risks of failure.

Transaction Costs

Transaction costs (cost of using vs selling unutilized resource to the third parties) – The transaction cost framework has been used to explain the boundaries of the firm.

🔹Transaction costs = costs of using the market: searching for a supplier, negotiating a contract, monitoring performance, enforcing terms.
🔹Boundaries of the firm = what the firm does itself vs. what it buys from the market (make or buy decision).
🔹The transaction cost framework (Coase, Williamson) explains why firms exist and why they diversify.

Efficiency-based arguments for diversification have to be compared with the alternative of using the market.

🔹Efficiency-based arguments = diversification saves costs, improves resource use, etc.
🔹Alternative = instead of diversifying, the firm could buy the product or service from an outside supplier.
🔹The comparison is: produce internally (diversify) vs. buy externally (stay focused).
🔹Diversification is only justified if internal production is cheaper/better than buying.

For diversification to yield competitive advantage requires not only the existence of economies of scope in common resources but also the presence of transaction costs that discourage them from selling or renting the use of the resource to other firms.

🔹Competitive advantage = doing something better than rivals, leading to higher profits.
🔹Economies of scope = cost savings from joint production – necessary but not sufficient.
🔹Transaction costs must also exist – if markets work perfectly, you could just rent out the resource.
🔹High transaction costs make selling impractical, so diversification becomes the better choice.

Transaction costs are likely to be substantial when intangible assets, such as brand names and technical knowledge, are involved.

🔹Intangible assets = non-physical valuable things (brands, patents, trade secrets, know-how).
🔹Brand names – difficult to "rent" because quality control is hard; a licensee could damage the brand.
🔹Technical knowledge – hard to sell without revealing the secret; contracts are hard to enforce.
🔹So firms diversify into related areas to exploit their intangible assets internally.

If the transaction costs of writing and enforcing contracts are greater than the governance costs, then the firm may find diversification the preferred option.

🔹Transaction costs of contracts = legal fees, time spent negotiating, risk of breach.
🔹Governance costs = costs of managing an internal division (supervision, coordination, bureaucracy).
🔹If transaction costs > governance costs → diversification (internal) wins.
🔹If governance costs > transaction costs → stay focused and use contracts.
📌 Key Insight: Transaction cost economics explains diversification as a response to market failures. When market contracts are costly, internal production (diversification) becomes more efficient.

Market Power

Market power – Diversification does not add to the market power of the firm in the sense that its market share is increased in a single market.

🔹Market power = ability to raise prices above competitive levels.
🔹Diversification spreads the firm across multiple markets – it does not automatically increase share in any one market.
🔹If you enter a new market, you start from zero share – no immediate market power there.
🔹So diversification alone is not the same as monopolization of a single market.

However, it does increase its ability to adopt other anti-competitive practices.

🔹Anti-competitive practices = actions that harm competition, like predatory pricing, cross-subsidization, exclusionary contracts.
🔹A diversified firm has more resources and more flexibility to engage in such practices.
🔹Example: A firm that makes profits in one market can use those profits to fund a price war in another market.
🔹This is the concern with conglomerate power – diversification can enable bad behavior.

The ability to do so comes from the strength of the company to finance activity in one market with support of profits made in another.

🔹Cross-subsidization = using profits from one division to cover losses in another.
🔹A single-product firm cannot do this – if it loses money, it has no other profit source.
🔹A diversified firm can sustain losses in one market for a long time, funded by profitable markets elsewhere.
🔹This gives it an unfair advantage over single-product competitors.

The implication is that diversified firms will thrive at the expense of non-diversified firms not because they are more efficient, but because they have access to what is termed conglomerate power, which is derived from the sum of its market power in individual markets.

🔹Conglomerate power = market power that comes from being diversified, not from dominance in any single market.
🔹Thrive at the expense of non-diversified firms = diversified firms may drive focused competitors out of business.
🔹Not because they are more efficient – but because they have deeper pockets and can cross-subsidize.
🔹This is a concern for antitrust policy.
📌 Key Insight: Diversification can create "conglomerate power" – the ability to engage in anti-competitive practices like cross-subsidization, giving diversified firms unfair advantages over focused competitors.
Chapter 5 | Slide 19
Benefits and Costs of Diversification

Benefits and Costs of Diversification – The benefits of diversification give the firm cost advantages for given ranges of output and revenue possibilities.

🔹Benefits = cost savings, risk reduction, revenue growth.
🔹"For given ranges of output" = at certain production levels, diversification lowers average costs.
🔹Not all output levels – benefits may exist only within certain ranges.
🔹Beyond those ranges, costs may rise – there is an optimal degree of diversification.

In practice, the firm has to combine cost advantages and disadvantages and determine the optimal degree of diversification that aids the maximization of profits.

🔹Cost advantages = economies of scope, shared resources, risk reduction.
🔹Cost disadvantages = increased complexity, coordination costs, bureaucracy.
🔹Optimal degree = the level of diversification where marginal benefit = marginal cost.
🔹Profit maximization = not just more diversification – but the right amount.

Diversification that initially leads to cost savings may later lead to cost increases; this is more likely to happen the further the firm moves from its core activities and the larger the firm becomes.

🔹Initially – first few diversification moves may save costs.
🔹Later – after a point, additional diversification raises costs.
🔹Further from core activities = unrelated diversification is more likely to increase costs.
🔹Larger the firm = diseconomies of scale, bureaucracy, coordination failures.

Cross-sectional studies show an inverted U-shaped relationship between profit and diversification. Profit initially increases, but the more diversified the company becomes so the rate of profit declines.

🔹Cross-sectional studies = comparing many different firms at one point in time.
🔹Inverted U-shaped = like an upside-down U: profits go up, reach a peak, then go down.
🔹The peak is the optimal level of diversification.
🔹Beyond that peak, adding more products or markets reduces profitability.
🔹This is one of the most robust findings in diversification research.

Another problem with increasing diversification is that shareholders and financial markets find it increasingly difficult to value the firm because of the wide range of activities, the disbelief in effective internal capital markets and the absence of appropriate valuation techniques for highly diversified firms.

🔹Difficult to value = investors cannot easily tell if the firm is overpriced or underpriced.
🔹Wide range of activities = a conglomerate with many unrelated businesses – each requires different valuation methods.
🔹Disbelief in effective internal capital markets = investors doubt that conglomerates allocate capital better than external markets.
🔹As a result, diversified firms often trade at a "conglomerate discount" – their stock price is lower than the sum of their parts.
📌 Key Insight: Diversification has an inverted U-shaped relationship with profitability – moderate diversification increases profits, but excessive diversification reduces them due to complexity and management costs.

INTEGRATION (Definition)

INTEGRATION – It refers to the operations by a firm in two or more industries representing successive stages in the flow of materials or products from an earlier to later stage of production or vice versa.

🔹Integration here means vertical integration – linking different stages of production.
🔹Successive stages = raw material → component manufacturing → assembly → wholesale → retail.
🔹From earlier to later (forward) or later to earlier (backward).
🔹Example: An oil company owns oil wells (early stage), refineries (middle stage), and gas stations (later stage).

Thus, it is a type of diversification but it may be looked as 'vertical concentration', and if the process takes place by merging of two different firms, then it is 'vertical merger'.

🔹A type of diversification – vertical diversification, as opposed to horizontal or conglomerate.
🔹Vertical concentration = control over multiple stages of the supply chain.
🔹If achieved through merger (two separate firms combining), it's a vertical merger.
🔹If achieved through internal expansion (building new divisions), it's vertical integration without a merger.

However, vertical integration is a popular term for all these.

🔹"All these" = whether by internal growth or by merger, people call it vertical integration.
🔹The term is used broadly to mean single ownership of multiple stages.
🔹The method (build vs. buy) is important for strategy, but the result is similar.
🔹So vertical integration is the general concept.

Essentially, it is the integration among intermediate products used in production of a commodity.

🔹Intermediate products = goods that are inputs to further production (not sold to final consumers).
🔹Example: Steel is an intermediate product for car manufacturing.
🔹Integration among intermediate products = owning the steel mill and the car factory together.
🔹The final commodity (car) is the end product.

It may be initiated in either way, i.e., a firm itself starts manufacturing all of them or different firms producing goods at different stages of the process and merge together.

🔹Either way = two paths: Internal expansion (build) or merger (buy).
🔹The result is the same: vertical integration.
🔹The choice depends on costs, speed, and availability of targets.
🔹Both are common in business history.
📌 Key Definition: Vertical integration is the ownership of multiple successive stages of production. It can be achieved through internal expansion or merger.

Types of Integration

Types of Integration – Integration of firms may be either horizontal or vertical in nature, or conglomerate.

🔹This slide lists the three types, but then focuses on vertical integration.
🔹Horizontal integration = merging with competitors at the same stage (already discussed in diversification).
🔹Vertical integration = merging with firms at different stages (the focus here).
🔹Conglomerate integration = merging with unrelated firms (also discussed earlier).

Horizontal integration occurs when a business merges with or acquires another business. It is the acquisition of additional business activities at the same level of the value chain. Here, businesses in the same industry and which operate at the same stage of the production process are combined.

🔹Same level of the value chain = both make the same type of product.
🔹Example: Two car manufacturers merge – horizontal integration.
🔹The goal is often to increase market share, reduce competition, or achieve economies of scale.
🔹Horizontal integration is about becoming bigger in the same business, not moving into different stages.

Vertical integration is the process in which several steps in the production and/or distribution of a product or service are controlled by a single company or entity, in order to increase that company's or entity's power in the market place.

🔹Several steps = multiple stages of the supply chain.
🔹Controlled by a single company = ownership, not just contracts.
🔹Goal: increase market power – by controlling inputs or distribution channels.
🔹Example: A coffee company that grows beans, roasts them, and runs its own coffee shops is vertically integrated.

Backward vertical integration: involves acquiring a business operating earlier in the supply chain – e.g. a retailer buys a wholesaler, a brewer buys a hop farm.

🔹Earlier in the supply chain = closer to raw materials (upstream).
🔹Retailer buys wholesaler – moving backward toward distribution.
🔹Brewer buys hop farm – moving backward toward raw materials.
🔹Backward integration secures supply, controls quality, and may lower costs.

Forward vertical integration: involves acquiring a business further up in the supply chain – e.g. a vehicle manufacturer buys a car parts distributor

🔹Further up the supply chain = closer to the final customer (downstream).
🔹Vehicle manufacturer buys a distributor – moving forward toward retail.
🔹Another example: A farmer buys a restaurant chain – forward integration.
🔹Forward integration secures market access, captures retail profits, and controls brand presentation.

Conglomerate integration occurs when a business moves into a totally different area.

🔹Already covered earlier – unrelated diversification.
🔹Example: A tobacco company buys a hotel chain.
🔹No operational connection between old and new businesses.
🔹Motives are financial (risk spreading, internal capital markets) rather than operational.
📌 Key Types: Horizontal (same stage), Vertical (different stages – backward/forward), Conglomerate (unrelated). Vertical integration is the focus of this section.

Vertical Integration (Forward and Backward)

Vertical integration occurs in one of two ways; Forward vertical integration occurs when a business acquires another business, which brings it closer to the customer.

🔹Closer to the customer = moving downstream, toward the final buyer.
🔹Example: A clothing manufacturer buys a chain of retail clothing stores.
🔹The manufacturer now sells directly to consumers, capturing retail profits.
🔹Forward integration also gives control over how products are marketed and displayed.

Backward vertical integration move closer to its sources of supply.

🔹Closer to sources of supply = moving upstream, toward raw materials.
🔹Example: A furniture manufacturer buys a timber forest and a sawmill.
🔹Now it controls its wood supply – quality, cost, and availability.
🔹Backward integration protects against supplier price increases or shortages.

Vertical integration involves joining together under common ownership a series of separate but linked production processes.

🔹Separate but linked = different firms each doing one stage, but the stages connect sequentially.
🔹Joining under common ownership = one company owns all stages.
🔹Example: From iron ore mining to steel making to car manufacturing – all owned by one company.
🔹This is the essence of vertical integration.

Such a strategy is used by many enterprises to widen the boundaries of the firm and to enlarge its size.

🔹Widen boundaries = the firm now does more things internally that it used to buy from markets.
🔹Enlarge its size = more employees, more assets, more revenue.
🔹Vertical integration is a growth strategy, not just an efficiency strategy.
🔹Many firms vertically integrate simply to become larger and more powerful.

The production of goods and services involves a chain of linked activities from raw materials to final product.

🔹Chain of linked activities = the value chain or supply chain.
🔹Example (simple): Mining → smelting → manufacturing → assembly → distribution → retail.
🔹Each activity uses the output of the previous activity as input.
🔹Vertical integration means owning multiple links in that chain.

Vertical integration is the outcome of a make or buy decision.

🔹Make or buy decision = should we produce this input ourselves (make) or purchase it from an outside supplier (buy)?
🔹If the firm decides "make" for multiple stages, it becomes vertically integrated.
🔹If it decides "buy" for all stages except one, it remains specialized.
🔹The make-or-buy decision is the core of vertical integration strategy.
📌 Key Concept: Vertical integration is a make-or-buy decision. Backward integration secures inputs; forward integration secures market access.

Motives of Vertical Integration (Overview)

Motives of Vertical Integration – Firms may decide on a strategy of vertical integration for a multitude of reasons that do not lend themselves to neat economic categorizations.

🔹Multitude of reasons = many different motives, not just one.
🔹Do not lend themselves to neat economic categorizations = the reasons are messy, overlapping, and context-specific.
🔹Different industries, different times, different firms have different motives.
🔹So we need a broad framework, not a single simple theory.

🔹Four headings – a useful organizing framework.
🔹Technological joint economies – cost savings from physically linking stages (e.g., hot metal directly to rolling mill).
🔹Avoid imperfect markets – when markets fail, vertical integration solves them.
🔹Distribution cost savings – lower costs of moving products between stages.
🔹Security and planning – reduce uncertainty, ensure supply, stabilize production.

Porter suggested examining the advantages to a firm of pursuing a strategy of vertical integration under six headings: cost savings, increased control, improved communications, changed organizational climate, operations management and competitive differentiation.

🔹Michael Porter – famous strategy scholar (Five Forces, Value Chain).
🔹Cost savings – obvious.
🔹Increased control – over quality, timing, and quantity.
🔹Improved communications – easier information flow inside one firm.
🔹Operations management – scheduling and coordination are easier internally.
🔹Competitive differentiation – vertical integration can be a source of unique advantage.

Traditional Explanations for Vertical Integration

The traditional explanations for firms seeking to vertically integrate are: To establish a source of supply if none exists.

🔹If no supplier exists for a needed input, the firm must make it itself.
🔹Example: In a new industry, specialized components may not be available on the market.
🔹Henry Ford integrated backwards to make his own steel because no one made the type he needed.
🔹This is a necessity motive, not a choice.

To secure cost savings by bringing under single ownership technologically linked processes.

🔹Technologically linked processes = stages that naturally fit together physically or chemically.
🔹Example: Oil refining and petrochemical production – heat from refining can be used for chemical processes.
🔹Savings come from avoiding reheating, transport, or storage between stages.

To ensure the quality of the input.

🔹If outside suppliers provide inconsistent quality, vertical integration lets the firm control quality.
🔹Example: A luxury car maker may integrate into leather tanning to ensure perfect hides.
🔹Quality control is especially important when defects are hard to detect before use.
🔹Internal production allows real-time quality monitoring.

To weaken the position of a supplier who appears to be making excessive profits. Hence: To secure a supply of inputs at lower prices.

🔹If a supplier has monopoly power and charges high prices, backward integration is a threat.
🔹The threat of integration can force the supplier to lower prices.
🔹By integrating, the firm pays only its own production costs, not the supplier's profit margin.

To control retail outlets and ensure market presence.

🔹Forward integration into retail ensures that your products are available to customers.
🔹Example: A manufacturer opens its own stores (Apple Store, Tesla showroom).
🔹This prevents retailers from favoring competitors or dropping your product.
🔹It also gives direct customer feedback and brand control.

To strengthen monopoly power and raise barriers to entry.

🔹Vertical integration can increase market power by controlling key inputs or distribution channels.
🔹A vertically integrated firm can make it harder for new entrants: they would need to enter multiple stages at once.
🔹Example: A vertically integrated aluminum company owns bauxite mines, refineries, smelters, and fabricators – a new entrant would need to build all four.
🔹This raises barriers to entry significantly.
📌 Traditional Motives: Secure supply, cost savings, quality control, weaken suppliers, control retail, and raise entry barriers.

Technical Efficiency and Production Cost Savings

Technical efficiency and production cost savings – linking the production of an input and output through ownership produces a more cost-effective solution.

🔹Cost-effective solution = lower total cost than using separate firms.
🔹Linking through ownership = vertical integration.
🔹The argument: physical coordination is cheaper inside one firm than through market contracts.
🔹Example: A steel plant next to a pipe mill – hot steel goes directly to pipe forming, saving energy and transport.

Production cost economies resulting from locating successive stages of production next to each other do not necessarily require single ownership of each stage: independent firms will locate such plants close to the source of the input if there are significant gains to be made.

🔹This is a counterargument – you don't need ownership to get location economies.
🔹Independent firms can co-locate (e.g., a steel mill next to a pipe mill owned by different companies).
🔹They can sign long-term contracts to share the benefits.
🔹So ownership is not strictly necessary for physical coordination – transaction costs matter.

By avoiding the separate market, the integrated firm can avoid market transaction costs but does incur additional costs for managing a larger firm.

🔹Avoid market transaction costs = no search, negotiation, monitoring, enforcement costs.
🔹Incur additional management costs = more layers, more coordination, more bureaucracy.
🔹The decision to integrate depends on which is larger: transaction costs saved vs. management costs added.
🔹This is the core trade-off in transaction cost economics.

On the other hand, the increased complexity of the firm may increase management costs compared with separately owned operations.

🔹Increased complexity = more products, more stages, more people.
🔹Complexity can lead to confusion, delays, and mistakes.
🔹Separately owned operations have simpler management tasks.
🔹So integration may not always reduce total costs.
📌 Key Insight: Vertical integration saves transaction costs but adds management costs. The decision to integrate depends on which is larger – a fundamental trade-off.

Vertical Integration and Uncertainty

Vertical integration may reduce the uncertainties faced by non-integrated firms.

🔹Uncertainties = unpredictable events: supply disruptions, price spikes, demand changes.
🔹Non-integrated firms = firms that buy inputs from the market.
🔹Integration can shield a firm from market volatility.
🔹Example: A vertically integrated food company does not worry about crop price fluctuations because it owns its own farms.

The controller of a firm is a boundedly rational individual making decisions with imperfect information in an uncertain environment.

🔹Boundedly rational = limited ability to process information and foresee the future (Herbert Simon).
🔹Imperfect information = not knowing everything about the market or the future.
🔹This realistic view of human limits explains why firms seek to reduce uncertainty.

The sources of uncertainty in relation to supply include: Unexpected unreliability of suppliers to deliver on time and the consequences for production scheduling of losing critical supplies.

🔹Unexpected unreliability = a supplier that suddenly fails to deliver as promised.
🔹Consequences = factory downtime, missed orders, lost customers.
🔹Example: A car plant shuts down because a parts supplier had a strike.
🔹Vertical integration can eliminate this uncertainty – your own division will not strike against you.

Unexpected use of monopoly power by suppliers. Variable quality of input that affects quality of output.

🔹A supplier with monopoly power may suddenly raise prices.
🔹If a supplier's quality varies, your final product quality varies.
🔹Variable quality damages brand reputation and customer satisfaction.
🔹Owning the supplier lets you control quality at the source.

Vertical integration allows the firm to become more of a planning system.

🔹Planning system = instead of reacting to markets, the firm plans production internally.
🔹This is the opposite of the market system (supply and demand).
🔹A vertically integrated firm can set internal transfer prices and allocate resources by command.
🔹This reduces uncertainty and transaction costs but may reduce efficiency.
📌 Key Insight: Vertical integration reduces supply uncertainty, protects against supplier opportunism, and allows the firm to become a planned system rather than a market reactor.

Transaction Cost Economics and Vertical Integration

Transaction cost economics is a key component of the more recent ideas explaining the drivers of vertical integration.

🔹Transaction cost economics (TCE) – developed by Ronald Coase (1937) and Oliver Williamson (1970s-2000s).
🔹TCE explains why firms exist and why they integrate vertically.
🔹The core idea: firms replace markets when market transaction costs are high.

It is argued that vertical integration will result in: Savings in transaction costs by not using the market, whereas buying through the market involves: incurring costs in searching for suppliers, discovering prices; writing, agreeing and monitoring contracts.

🔹Searching for suppliers – time, effort, travel, networking.
🔹Discovering prices – negotiating, comparing bids, market research.
🔹Writing contracts – legal fees, specifying terms, contingencies.
🔹Agreeing – bargaining, concessions, delays.
🔹Monitoring contracts – inspections, audits, tracking performance.
🔹All these costs are avoided if the firm makes the input internally.

Increasing management costs because internalized activities will require supervision and co-ordination.

🔹Internal activities are not free – they require managers.
🔹Supervising workers, coordinating across departments, resolving conflicts.
🔹Management costs = salaries of middle managers, IT systems, meeting time.
🔹These are the costs of "governance" inside the firm.

Thus, the increase in management cost has to be less than the savings in transaction costs to justify vertical integration and also avoids problems associated with contracts.

🔹The decision rule: Integrate if saved transaction costs > added management costs.
🔹Also avoids problems associated with contracts = disputes, renegotiation, legal fees.
🔹This is the key formula of transaction cost economics.

If suppliers have invested in highly specialized assets to produce the required input, then they may be able to exploit this to negotiate a higher price.

🔹Highly specialized assets = equipment, training, or facilities that are useless except for one specific buyer.
🔹Example: A supplier builds a factory next to the buyer's plant, customized to the buyer's specifications.
🔹After that investment, the buyer is locked in – switching suppliers would cost a fortune.
🔹The supplier can then demand higher prices (hold-up problem).
🔹Vertical integration solves this by owning the specialized asset.
📌 Key Insight: Transaction cost economics explains vertical integration as a response to high market transaction costs and the risk of opportunistic behavior (hold-up problem) with specialized assets.

Vertical Integration and Profitability

Vertical Integration and Profitability – Lessons learned from several vertically integrated acquisitions indicate that the corporate parent's influence over the acquired business is the primary factor determining success or failure.

🔹Corporate parent = the acquiring company, the new owner.
🔹Influence = how much and how well the parent manages the acquired business.
🔹Primary factor = more important than the industry, the size, or the technology.
🔹If influence is skillful and appropriate, success; if not, failure.

For this to have a favourable impact: The acquired business must have the potential to improve its performance independently of its relationships with other divisions or business units within the company.

🔹Favourable impact = successful vertical integration.
🔹Potential to improve on its own = the business is not hopeless.
🔹It must stand on its own feet eventually.

The parent company must have the skills or resources necessary to help the business. In practice, they may not have the skills, and the methods chosen to integrate the company may cause more problems than they solve.

🔹Skills or resources – the parent must actually know how to improve the business.
🔹In practice, parents often overestimate their abilities.
🔹Wrong integration methods = forcing the acquired company to adopt the parent's systems, even if inappropriate.
🔹More problems than solve = the cure is worse than the disease.

The parent company must understand the business well enough to avoid influencing it in ways that damage its performance.

🔹Understand well enough = not just general management, but specific industry knowledge.
🔹Avoiding damage = not imposing bad policies, not cutting useful functions, not alienating key employees.
🔹Many vertical integrations fail because the parent interferes badly.
🔹So vertical integration is not automatically beneficial – it requires skillful parenting.
📌 Key Insight: Vertical integration success depends primarily on the parent company's ability to manage the acquired business effectively, not just the industry or size.

MERGERS (Definition and Nature)

MERGERS – This term refers to the amalgamation or integration of two or more firms.

🔹Amalgamation = combining into one.
🔹Two or more firms join to become a single legal entity.
🔹Merger is a form of consolidation of market power.
🔹In a merger, the firms are usually roughly equal in size (unlike a takeover).

The firms under different ownership and management controls come under a united one through merger.

🔹Different ownership and management = separate companies before.
🔹United one = single company after.
🔹Control is transferred from multiple groups to one management team.
🔹This is a fundamental change in market structure.

The terms 'acquisition' and 'takeover' are also used for 'merger', which implies that a firm acquires assets or stocks in part or full, of other firm(s) to get operational control over them.

🔹Acquisition / takeover – often used interchangeably with merger.
🔹Acquires assets or stocks = buys the target company's physical assets or its shares.
🔹Operational control = the power to make decisions about the target's business.
🔹In an acquisition, one firm is clearly the buyer; in a merger, they may be equals.

In legal sense, there is a difference between these terms but from the point of view of the economic analysis they are similar.

🔹Legal sense – mergers require agreement of both boards; takeovers can be hostile.
🔹Economic analysis focuses on the result: transfer of control and combination of assets.
🔹For economists, the distinction is less important than the effects.

The important feature of merger, that is relevant to us, is the transfer of control of business activity from one or more firms to another.

🔹Transfer of control – the key economic event.
🔹Control means the ability to direct resources, set strategy, hire/fire managers.
🔹This transfer of control is what changes market concentration and competition.
📌 Key Definition: A merger is the combination of two or more firms into a single entity. Acquisitions and takeovers are similar but with different legal and strategic characteristics.
Chapter 5 | Slide 30
Types of Mergers

Types of Mergers – Economists have identified three types of mergers formed by firms. These are: Horizontal mergers, Vertical mergers and Conglomerate mergers.

🔹This classification matches the three types of integration.
🔹Horizontal – same industry, same stage.
🔹Vertical – different stages of the same production chain.
🔹Conglomerate – unrelated industries.
🔹Each has different competitive implications and antitrust treatment.

Horizontal mergers occur when two firms in the same market are consolidated into a single enterprise; this means that the new enterprise will have increased its market share.

🔹Same market = direct competitors.
🔹Consolidated = combined into one.
🔹Increased market share = the merged firm has a larger share than either had separately.
🔹This type of merger is designed to acquire market power.
🔹Horizontal mergers are most closely scrutinized by antitrust authorities.

Vertical mergers take place between firms, which engage in successive stages of production such as brewing and running public houses; so both upstream (backward) or downstream (forward) are possible.

🔹Successive stages = one firm's output is another firm's input.
🔹Example: Brewery (makes beer) and pub chain (sells beer to customers).
🔹Upstream (backward) = merging with a supplier.
🔹Downstream (forward) = merging with a customer.

Conglomerate mergers occur when two firms producing independent products for different markets merge. Conglomerate mergers create larger diversified firms.

🔹Independent products = no customer or technology relationship.
🔹Different markets = no market overlap.
🔹Example: A cement company merges with a software company.
🔹The result is a larger, diversified (conglomerate) firm.

Although these do not generate concerns about market dominance, there are concerns about their ability to compete unfairly against undiversified competitors because of their ability to cross-subsidize.

🔹No concerns about market dominance – no single market becomes more concentrated.
🔹Concerns about unfair competition – the conglomerate can use profits from one market to fund predatory pricing in another.
🔹Cross-subsidize = support losses in one division with profits from another.
🔹Undiversified competitors cannot do this – they may be driven out.
📌 Three Types of Mergers: Horizontal (competitors), Vertical (different stages), Conglomerate (unrelated). Each has different competitive effects and antitrust implications.

Motives for Merging (Overview)

Motives for Merging – The motives for merging are different in managerial and owner-controlled firms: The former may be more concerned with increasing the growth rate of the firm, while owners are presumed to be more concerned with increasing profits or shareholder value.

🔹Managerial-controlled firms – run by professional managers who own few shares.
🔹Their motive: growth (higher sales, more employees, larger assets) – because their pay and status are tied to size.
🔹Owner-controlled firms – run by owners or owner-representatives.
🔹Their motive: profit and shareholder value – because their wealth is tied to returns.
🔹This is a classic principal-agent difference in merger motives.

There are different causes/motives for mergers, among which, the following are included: Those relating to the structure of markets, i.e. the pursuit of either of economies of scale or of market power; Those which center on management efficiency; Theories based on the tax effects of merger.

🔹Market structure motives – economies of scale (cost savings) or market power (higher prices).
🔹Management efficiency motives – replacing inefficient managers, improving performance.
🔹Tax effects – using losses to reduce tax, changing capital structure for tax benefits.
🔹These three categories will be explained in the next slides.
📌 Key Insight: Merger motives differ between managers (growth-focused) and owners (profit-focused). Main categories: market structure (scale/power), management efficiency, and tax effects.
Chapter 5 | Slide 32
Economies of Scale: Efficiency Gain

Economies of scale: Efficiency Gain – The first efficiency argument is the advantages of economies of scale.

🔹This is the most straightforward motive: combining two firms can lower average costs.
🔹Fixed costs (R&D, headquarters, factories) are spread over more output.
🔹Bulk purchasing, specialized machinery, and division of labor improve.
🔹Economies of scale are a purely efficiency-based reason for merging.

There are assets, which are costly, indivisible and 'fungible' (that is capable of being used in several industries).

🔹Costly – expensive to acquire.
🔹Indivisible – cannot be bought in small pieces (e.g., a supercomputer, a brand, a patent).
🔹Fungible – can be used in multiple different industries or applications.
🔹Example: A powerful AI algorithm can be used in healthcare, finance, and autonomous vehicles – fungible.

Such assets give rise to 'economies of scope': they make it relatively easier for a company to enter new lines of business.

🔹Economies of scope – cheaper to produce multiple products together than separately.
🔹A fungible asset (like a brand or a technology) can be applied to many products.
🔹This makes diversification easier – the firm can enter new lines without building new assets from scratch.
🔹Example: Amazon's cloud computing infrastructure (AWS) was built for its own e-commerce but then sold to other companies – economies of scope.

When fungible assets are the motive for merger, the result is likely to be diversification.

🔹If a firm merges to gain access to a fungible asset (e.g., a brand, a patent, a platform), it will likely use that asset to enter new markets.
🔹The merger is not just about scale in one market – it's about scope across markets.
🔹So the result is a more diversified firm.
🔹Example: A beverage company buys a snack company to use its distribution network for both products.
📌 Key Insight: Economies of scale (lower average costs) and economies of scope (cost savings from joint production) are efficiency-based motives for mergers and diversification.
Chapter 5 | Slide 33
Management Performance: Market for Corporate Control

Management Performance: Merger as the Outcome of 'Market of Corporate Control' – The underlying theory of this outlook lies in both the 'managerial' and 'principal-agent' theories of the firm.

🔹Market for corporate control – the idea that companies are bought and sold like any other asset.
🔹Poorly managed companies become takeover targets.
🔹Managerial theory of the firm – managers pursue their own goals, not shareholders'.
🔹Mergers are a way to discipline poor managers.

This is all about 'market for corporate control' that may lead to hostile take-over.

🔹Hostile takeover = acquiring a company against the wishes of its management.
🔹The market for corporate control allows outsiders to replace bad managers by buying the company.
🔹This is a "market" – buyers bid for control, sellers (shareholders) accept.
🔹Hostile takeovers are the ultimate threat to underperforming managers.

The implication of this competition in management is the fact that this spurs efficiency.

🔹Spurs efficiency = encourages managers to perform better.
🔹If managers run the company poorly, its share price falls → the company becomes a cheap takeover target.
🔹Fear of takeover forces managers to maximize profits and share price.
🔹This is the disciplinary effect of the market for corporate control.

If managers fail to maximize profits they lay themselves open to takeover.

🔹Fail to maximize profits – pursue growth, perks, or quiet life instead of profits.
🔹Lay themselves open = become vulnerable.
🔹A low share price invites raiders.
🔹Once taken over, the managers are likely fired.
🔹So the threat of takeover is a powerful motivator.

One of the critics forwarded against this role of mergers is that of short-termism.

🔹Short-termism – focusing on short-term profits at the expense of long-term value.
🔹Managers fearing takeover may cut R&D, advertising, or capital investment to boost current profits.
🔹This makes the company look good now but hurts it later.
🔹So the threat of takeover can actually harm long-term efficiency.
📌 Key Insight: The market for corporate control disciplines underperforming managers, but may also encourage short-termism at the expense of long-term value.

Taxation Effect of Merger

3. Taxation effect of merger – Merging may have the effect of reducing the aggregate tax liability of the companies concerned.

🔹Aggregate tax liability = total taxes paid by the merged company compared to the two separate companies.
🔹Tax reduction is a financial benefit of merging.
🔹It is not an efficiency gain – it's a transfer from the government to the firm.

Tax authorities treat transactions differently; Since interest payments on companies' borrowed funds are tax-deductible there is an incentive to issue bonds against shares.

🔹Interest payments are tax-deductible – they reduce taxable profit.
🔹Dividends are not tax-deductible – paid from after-tax profit.
🔹So debt financing has a tax advantage over equity financing.

Second, while company profits are taxed, losses entitle a company to a refund or a reduction in future tax.

🔹Losses – negative profits.
🔹Tax refund or reduction – many tax systems allow loss carry-backs or carry-forwards.
🔹A profitable company can acquire a loss-making company and use its tax losses.
🔹This is a classic tax-driven merger motive.

This feature of the tax system in some countries means that a profitable raider, which acquires a victim with tax losses can use those losses to reduce its own tax liabilities.

🔹Profitable raider – acquiring company with high profits and high taxes.
🔹Victim with tax losses – target company losing money, with unused loss carry-forwards.
🔹Acquire the victim → use its losses to offset raider's profits → lower tax bill.
🔹This is a pure tax arbitrage – no real economic benefit, just tax avoidance.
📌 Key Insight: Tax motives for mergers include using debt's tax advantage and acquiring loss-making companies to offset taxable profits.

Mergers and Growth

Mergers and growth – Marris (1964) in his analysis of growth visualized the firm having to create opportunities for growth to satisfy managerial preferences.

🔹Marris (1964) – economist Robin Marris, known for growth theory of the firm.
🔹Managerial preferences – managers like growth (higher salaries, power, prestige).
🔹If the firm cannot grow internally (limited market for existing products), it must create opportunities.
🔹Mergers are one way to create growth opportunities.

If the firm is limited in its growth opportunities in its existing activities, then the acquisition of other enterprises is one way of increasing its size and increasing its average growth rate as long as the acquired activity is in a faster growing sector.

🔹Limited growth opportunities – slow market growth, saturated demand.
🔹Acquisition of other enterprises = buying another company.
🔹Faster growing sector = the target operates in a market growing faster than the acquirer's market.
🔹Even if the acquirer's market grows at 2%, if the target's market grows at 10%, combined growth rate rises.

Acquisition is viewed as a more rapid way of achieving greater size and a higher growth rate than pursuing internal or organic growth.

🔹Internal/organic growth – building new factories, developing new products from scratch.
🔹This is slow – takes years.
🔹Acquisition is instant – you buy an existing company with existing sales.
🔹For managers wanting fast growth, acquisition is attractive.
📌 Key Insight: Mergers offer rapid growth compared to organic expansion, especially when current markets are saturated or slow-growing.
Chapter 5 | Slide 36
Mergers and Market Power

Mergers and market power – Market power arises from a firm having a significant presence in a market.

🔹Significant presence = large market share, strong brand, control over key resources.
🔹Market power allows raising prices above competitive levels.
🔹Mergers can increase market power.
🔹This is the most controversial motive for mergers.

Greater market power can be achieved by increasing market share at the expense of rivals.

🔹Increase market share organically (by competing better) – slow and uncertain.
🔹Or acquire a rival – immediate increase in market share.
🔹At the expense of rivals = they lose market share to you (or disappear).
🔹Higher market share → more market power.

The second way of achieving a higher market share is to acquire a rival; this eliminates a competitor and at the same time increases the market share of the acquiring firm.

🔹First way – win share by competing (organic).
🔹Second way – buy a rival (acquisition).
🔹Eliminates a competitor – one less rival to worry about.
🔹Increases acquirer's share immediately – the target's customers become the acquirer's customers.
📌 Key Insight: Mergers can increase market power directly by eliminating rivals and increasing market share, which is why horizontal mergers face antitrust scrutiny.

Acquiring Competences

Acquiring competences – A firm may be motivated to acquire another because of the assets the target firm possesses.

🔹Not all acquisitions are about market share or cost savings.
🔹Some are about acquiring valuable assets that are hard to buy separately.
🔹Target's assets = patents, technology, brand, customer relationships, skilled workforce.
🔹This is called an "asset-seeking" or "capability-seeking" acquisition.

In particular, the concern is to acquire intangible assets or competences that cannot be purchased in the market.

🔹Intangible assets – non-physical: knowledge, brand, culture, processes.
🔹Cannot be purchased in the market – you cannot just order "Toyota's production system" from a catalog.
🔹The only way to get them is to buy the whole company.
🔹Example: Google acquired Android not for its revenue (zero at the time) but for its mobile OS competence.

Such assets are embedded in individuals and the architecture of the firm.

🔹Embedded in individuals – specific people carry the knowledge (engineers, managers, designers).
🔹Architecture of the firm – routines, culture, communication patterns, teamwork.
🔹You cannot separate the asset from the people and the organization.
🔹If you hire the individuals but not the firm, the architecture is lost.

It is these competences that make a firm potentially more profitable than its competitors, but it is also these competences that make the firm a potential target.

🔹Competences = source of superior profitability.
🔹That profitability makes the firm attractive to buyers.
🔹Rivals may want to acquire the firm to gain those competences.
🔹So the very thing that makes a firm successful also makes it a takeover target.

The problem with acquiring a firm for its competences is that they reside in one or more individuals; so, if they leave after the acquisition, then the takeover may have been in vain.

🔹In vain = wasted effort, no benefit.
🔹Key individuals may leave after acquisition – they don't like the new management, or they were paid and leave.
🔹Without those people, the competence walks out the door.
🔹This is the "key person risk" in competence-seeking acquisitions.
📌 Key Insight: Firms acquire others to gain intangible assets and competences (technology, brand, culture) that cannot be purchased separately, but face "key person risk" if talent leaves.

Mergers and Cost Savings

Mergers and cost savings – The majority of mergers are intended to produce cost savings from synergy between existing and acquired activities.

🔹Majority – most mergers have cost-saving as a primary motive.
🔹Synergy = 2 + 2 = 5 – combined value greater than sum of parts.
🔹Cost savings are the most concrete and measurable synergy.
🔹Mergers that fail often overestimated the cost savings.

The main source of these advantages will be: From economies of scale, as production is concentrated at fewer facilities; From economies of scope, as administrative functions are shared and purchases of raw materials are co-ordinated; and From economies of size, which allows larger firms to achieve lower costs than smaller ones.

🔹Economies of scale – produce more at the same factory → lower unit cost.
🔹Economies of scope – share functions (HR, IT, accounting) across divisions → lower overhead.
🔹Economies of size – bulk purchasing, lower borrowing costs, spreading fixed costs.
🔹These are the three cost-saving channels.

Whether the expected cost savings are achieved depends on the success of the acquiring firm to integrate the new operation into its existing organizational and management structure.

🔹Expected cost savings = what the merger plan promised.
🔹Integration is difficult – merging different cultures, systems, and processes.
🔹If integration fails, cost savings may never materialize.
🔹This is why many mergers fail to deliver the promised benefits.

If the costs of restructuring and setting up new management structures prove more expensive than anticipated, then the merger may not achieve its expected benefits.

🔹Restructuring costs = severance, plant closures, system conversions, rebranding.
🔹More expensive than anticipated = cost overruns, delays, unexpected problems.
🔹Expected benefits = cost savings that justified the merger.
🔹If costs exceed savings, the merger destroys value.
📌 Key Insight: Cost savings from mergers come from economies of scale, scope, and size, but are only realized if integration is successful – many mergers fail to deliver promised savings.

Defensive and Opportunistic Reasons

Defensive and opportunistic reasons – The management of a firm may seek to merge for defensive reasons, such as to protect their own positions, to avoid bankruptcy or to avoid being taken over by an unwelcome bidder.

🔹Defensive reasons – protecting management's own jobs and control.
🔹Protect their own positions – make themselves harder to fire.
🔹Avoid bankruptcy – merge with a stronger firm to survive.
🔹Avoid unwelcome takeover – merge with a "white knight" (friendly acquirer) instead.

Alternatively, an acquisition may be made because a company becomes available.

🔹Opportunistic reasons – taking advantage of an opportunity when it appears.
🔹A company becomes available = for sale at a good price, or owner wants to retire.
🔹The acquirer was not actively seeking, but the opportunity was too good to pass.
🔹This is opportunistic, not strategic.

If a firm fears that it will become the subject of a takeover bid, then it may itself launch a bid to increase its size and make the firm a more expensive target.

🔹Fear of being taken over – the firm is a vulnerable target.
🔹Launch a bid = acquire another company (become a hunter instead of prey).
🔹Increasing size makes the firm more expensive to acquire – deters raiders.
🔹This is "defensive acquisition" or "shark repellent."

An alternative approach to such a threat or to a launched bid is to seek another firm, or suitor, of the firm's own choosing to take the firm over in preference to the original bid.

🔹Alternative approach – instead of acquiring someone else, find a friendly acquirer.
🔹Suitor of the firm's own choosing – a "white knight."
🔹The white knight agrees to acquire the firm on terms management prefers.
🔹This blocks the hostile raider (the original bidder).

Two smaller firms in a market might merge to create a stronger firm to survive the challenge of a larger rival.

🔹Survive the challenge – a large rival is dominating the market.
🔹Two small firms merge → become a medium firm → better able to compete.
🔹This is a defensive merger to avoid being crushed.
🔹Example: Two regional airlines merge to compete with a national giant.
📌 Key Insight: Defensive mergers aim to protect management or avoid takeover (white knights, shark repellent). Opportunistic mergers capitalize on unexpected opportunities.

Changes in the Economy and Final Summary

Changes in the economy – Mergers are sometimes motivated by general changes in an industry, such as changes in demand and technology, and trends in the economy as a whole, such as globalization.

🔹General changes – big, slow-moving trends, not firm-specific events.
🔹Changes in demand – tastes shift, new customer segments emerge.
🔹Changes in technology – new production methods, new product possibilities.
🔹Globalization – companies need scale to compete internationally.

For example, declining demand in the defence sector following the end of the cold war led to mergers of defence companies and consolidation of the industry.

🔹End of the Cold War (1991) – sharp reduction in military spending.
🔹Declining demand → many defense firms faced ruin.
🔹Mergers allowed them to cut costs, share facilities, and survive.
🔹Examples: Lockheed Martin, Boeing, Raytheon formed through mergers.

Changes in particular economic policies may create opportunities for merger activity as previous restrictions on firm behaviour are removed.

🔹Economic policies – deregulation, privatization, trade liberalization.
🔹Previous restrictions removed → new opportunities to merge.
🔹Example: Deregulation of airlines allowed them to merge across routes.
🔹Privatization of state-owned enterprises often leads to mergers with private firms.

Profit and efficiency benefits – The economic case for horizontal mergers is generally based on higher unit revenues from the use of market power and lower unit costs from efficiency savings.

🔹Higher unit revenues – raising prices (market power).
🔹Lower unit costs – economies of scale, scope, or size (efficiency).
🔹These are the two potential benefits of horizontal mergers.
🔹Antitrust authorities worry about the first (higher prices) but accept the second (cost savings).

For vertical mergers there may be cost savings where two technologically linked stages of a production chain are joined together under common ownership.

🔹Vertical mergers are less about market power and more about efficiency.
🔹Technologically linked stages = natural fit (e.g., assembly and parts manufacturing).
🔹Cost savings come from coordination, reduced inventory, lower transaction costs.

For conglomerate mergers where the activities are unrelated, the cost savings may arise from more efficient management, from a lower cost of capital for market funding and from operating an internal capital market.

🔹Conglomerate mergers have no operational synergies – no shared technology or customers.
🔹More efficient management – better managers run the acquired firm.
🔹Lower cost of capital – larger firm may borrow more cheaply.
🔹Internal capital market – the conglomerate can shift money from cash-rich divisions to investment-needy divisions without going through banks.
🔹These are financial and managerial synergies, not operational.
📌 Chapter 5 Summary: Diversification (concentric, horizontal, conglomerate) spreads risk and leverages resources. Vertical integration controls supply chains. Mergers (horizontal, vertical, conglomerate) are driven by efficiency, market power, growth, tax benefits, and defensive motives. Each strategy has distinct benefits and risks.

The Nature and Roles of Advertising

CHAPTER SIX – ADVERTISEMENT, RESEARCH & DEVELOPMENT AND TECHNOLOGICAL PROGRESS

Aims to put a product/service in the spotlight in hopes of drawing attentions from consumers.

🔹Put in the spotlight = make it visible, prominent, memorable.
🔹Hopes of drawing attention = no guarantee – advertising is an investment with uncertain returns.
🔹Attention is the first step toward purchase – if consumers don't notice, they won't buy.
🔹This is the basic, commonsense definition of advertising.

The term ''advertising'' is expenditure undertaken by a firm to promote the sales of its products or services.

🔹Expenditure = money spent, a cost to the firm.

🔹Advertising is an investment in demand creation.

Forms of advertising: The most visible form of advertising is paid-for space in print, radio/television, social medias.

🔹Paid-for space = the firm buys access to media channels.

🔹Promotional activity = broader than just media ads.

Nature of Advertisement (Informative vs. Persuasive)

Nature of advertisement – Economists distinguish two nature or roles of advertising.

🔹Economists classify advertising into two main types based on its purpose.
🔹This is a conceptual distinction – in reality, most ads mix both.
🔹The distinction is important for policy: informative ads are generally seen as good, persuasive ads as potentially problematic.

Informative advertisement: it involves with the provision of factual information to consumers about the characteristics of a product, its price and its availability.

🔹Factual information = objective, verifiable, useful for decision-making.
🔹Characteristics = size, weight, color, features, ingredients, performance.
🔹Price = how much it costs, sale prices, discounts.
🔹Availability = where to buy, when it's in stock, delivery options.

Such advertising helps consumers overcome information deficiencies.

🔹Information deficiencies = consumers don't know enough to make good choices.
🔹Without information, consumers may buy inferior products or pay too much.
🔹Informative advertising reduces this problem – it educates consumers.
🔹This is why economists generally like informative advertising – it improves market efficiency.

Persuasive advertisement: it is the persuasion of consumers to buy a particular product or visit a particular shop or restaurant, by emphasizing the qualities of the product or associating the product with a particular life style or celebrity.

🔹Persuasion = changing attitudes, not just providing facts.
🔹Emphasizing qualities = making the product seem better, even if not objectively different.
🔹Associating with lifestyle = linking product to happiness, success, sophistication.
🔹Celebrity endorsements = using famous people to lend credibility and glamour.

The goal of this type of advertising is to make the advertised firm's product appear superior by comparing one producer's product to those of competitors.

🔹Appear superior = create perception of superiority, even if real differences are small.
🔹Comparative advertising = "our product is better than theirs".
🔹The goal is brand preference, not just brand awareness.

Which one is good? The implicit assumption: is that informative advertising is good for the consumer, while persuasive advertising is not; though in practice it may be hard to distinguish between the two.

🔹Good vs. not good = from consumer welfare perspective.
🔹Informative → helps consumers make better choices → good.
🔹Persuasive → manipulates consumers, may lead to overpaying → questionable.
🔹In practice, most ads mix information and persuasion – hard to separate.
📌 Key Distinction: Informative advertising provides facts that help consumers make better choices. Persuasive advertising aims to change attitudes and create brand loyalty.

Why Firms Promote Their Products

Why firms promote its products? To alter consumer preference: First, they try to change consumer preferences by persuading consumers of the superior quality of their product by providing information about it and by promoting brand loyalty.

🔹Alter consumer preference = change what consumers like, not just what they know.
🔹Persuading of superior quality = convincing consumers that your product is better.
🔹Brand loyalty = making consumers prefer your brand habitually.

As a consequence, the firm promotes extra sales or is able to sell its product at a higher price.

🔹Two possible outcomes: sell more (higher quantity) or charge more (higher price).
🔹Extra sales = demand curve shifts right.
🔹Higher price = demand curve becomes steeper (less elastic).

In addition, the firm may be able to lower average costs of production by producing and selling more output, thereby increasing profits.

🔹Lower average costs = if the firm has economies of scale.
🔹Selling more output spreads fixed costs over more units.
🔹This is a second source of profit increase from advertising.

Advertising is designed to alter the consumer's preferences in favor of advertised products and against non-advertised products.

🔹In favor of advertised products = increase demand for your product.
🔹Against non-advertised products = decrease demand for rivals' products.
🔹This is competitive – gains for one firm often come at others' expense.

Advertising is often the principal method employed by firms to increase perceived differences between products among consumers and to create brand loyalty.

🔹Perceived differences = differences that exist in consumers' minds, whether real or not.
🔹Brand loyalty = consumers keep buying the same brand even when cheaper alternatives exist.
🔹This reduces price sensitivity and makes demand more inelastic.

In some oligopolistic markets, variations in advertising expenditure are thought to be more important than lowering price in trying to sell more of a product.

🔹In some markets, price cuts lead to immediate retaliation (price war).
🔹Advertising variation is less easily matched or less destructive.
🔹This is the "non-price competition" typical of oligopoly.

Advertising is undertaken to stimulate demand and, thereby, lower the price elasticity of demand for the product.

🔹Stimulate demand = shift demand curve right.
🔹Lower price elasticity = make demand steeper (less responsive to price changes).
🔹A less elastic demand allows the firm to raise price without losing many customers.
📌 Key Insight: Advertising can increase sales, enable higher prices, and lower average costs through economies of scale – potentially increasing profits through multiple channels.

Effectiveness of Advertising as a Market Weapon

Effectiveness of Advertising as a Market Weapon – Many theories believe that there are a number of interconnected advertising impacts that start with learning and attitude changes, move through the stages of purchase and repurchase, and ultimately have an effect on a company's sales and profits.

🔹Interconnected impacts = a chain of effects, not a single outcome.
🔹Learning → attitude change → purchase → repurchase → profit.
🔹This is the "hierarchy of effects" model in marketing.

There are several basic functions that advertising performs in marketing a product to a consumer.

🔹Each function addresses a different consumer psychology barrier.
🔹Functions: Familiarise, Reminding, Spreading news, Overcoming inertia, Adding value.

Familiarise: making a product well known to the consumer. This minimises the fear of the unknown, by reducing uncertainty.

🔹Familiarise = make the product known, recognizable.
🔹Familiarity reduces fear – known brands feel safer.

Reminding: re-announcing the brand's intrinsic values to consumers who already use it.

🔹Reminding = for existing customers, not new ones.
🔹Intrinsic values = quality, reliability, value, convenience.
🔹Prevents brand switching by keeping your brand salient.

Spreading news: this is the stereotyped role of advertising, announcing new products, changes in the existing product, price reductions and new sizes or colours.

🔹Stereotyped role = what most people think of as advertising.
🔹News = information that consumers didn't previously have.

Overcoming consumer's inertia: Consumer inertia is a term used to describe a fixed consumption model in which customers unintentionally shop at the same location or choose the same brand of goods based on prior consumption experience or purchases.

🔹Consumer inertia = sticking with the same brand out of habit, not loyalty.
🔹Inertia is a barrier to switching – advertising breaks the habit loop.

Adding a value not in the product: it may be argued that advertising creates genuine and real values that are, nevertheless, purely subjective. This is not surprisingly the most controversial function of advertising.

🔹Adding value not in the product = product physically unchanged, but perceived value higher.
🔹Subjective value = in the consumer's mind, not in the physical object.
🔹Example: A watch tells time – but a Rolex ad adds "status" and "achievement."
📌 Key Insight: Advertising works through multiple functions: familiarizing, reminding, spreading news, overcoming inertia, and adding subjective value.

The Persuasive View of Advertising

The Persuasive View – The persuasive view holds that advertising primarily affects demand by changing tastes and creating brand loyalty.

🔹Changing tastes = altering what consumers like, not just informing them.
🔹Creating brand loyalty = making consumers prefer a specific brand over others.
🔹The product may not be objectively better – but consumers think it is.

The advertised product thus faces a less elastic demand due to created brand loyalty.

🔹Less elastic demand = demand curve becomes steeper.
🔹Consumers are less likely to switch when price rises.
🔹This is how advertising generates market power.

This elasticity effect suggests that advertising causes higher prices, though this influence may be moderated by the presence of production scale economies.

🔹Causes higher prices = because demand is less elastic, firms raise price.
🔹Moderated = reduced, offset by scale economies.
🔹If advertising also increases sales and lowers costs, prices might not rise.

The persuasive view holds further that advertising may blocked/deter entry.

🔹Blocked/deter entry = prevent new firms from entering the market.
🔹Established firms with strong brand loyalty make it hard for new entrants.
🔹New firms must spend heavily on advertising just to be noticed.

Consumers are reluctant to try new products of unknown quality, and this experience-based asymmetry between established and new products may be aggravated in the presence of heavy advertising by established firms.

🔹Reluctant to try new products = consumers prefer known brands.
🔹Experience-based asymmetry = established brands have a history, new brands don't.
🔹Heavy advertising by established firms makes this asymmetry worse.
📌 Persuasive View: Advertising changes tastes, creates brand loyalty, makes demand less elastic, enables higher prices, and can deter new entry – raising concerns about market power.

The Informative View of Advertising

The Informative View – The informative view holds that advertising affects demand by conveying information.

🔹Conveying information = providing facts that help consumers make better choices.
🔹This view sees advertising as educational, not manipulative.
🔹Informed consumers are empowered consumers.

The advertised product thus faces a more elastic demand.

🔹More elastic demand = demand curve becomes flatter.
🔹Informed consumers can easily compare prices and features.
🔹They will switch to cheaper or better products readily.

Buyers comes more informed, then the market becomes more of competitive nature.

🔹More informed buyers → better choices → more competition.
🔹Firms cannot exploit consumer ignorance.
🔹Prices tend toward competitive levels.

This elasticity effect suggests that advertising causes lower prices, an influence which is reinforced when production scale economies are present.

🔹Causes lower prices = because demand is more elastic, firms compete on price.
🔹Reinforced by scale economies = lower costs allow even lower prices.
🔹Opposite of the persuasive view.

The informative view suggests further that advertised products are generally of high quality due to competitive nature.

🔹Generally of high quality = only good products survive in competitive markets.
🔹If a product is low quality, informed consumers won't buy it.
🔹So advertised products tend to be good products.

The informative view holds further that advertising is not used by established firms to deter entry; instead, advertising facilitates entry, since it is an important means through which entrants provide price and quality information to consumers.

🔹Facilitates entry = helps new firms enter.
🔹New firms can use advertising to announce themselves.
🔹Without advertising, consumers might never know the new firm exists.
📌 Informative View: Advertising provides useful information, makes demand more elastic, lowers prices, and facilitates entry – enhancing market efficiency and consumer welfare.

The Complementary View of Advertising

The Complementary View – Advertising primarily affects demand by exerting a complementary influence in the consumer's utility function with the consumption of the advertised product.

🔹Complementary influence = advertising itself is a "good" that adds value when consumed with the product.
🔹Utility function = the mathematical representation of consumer satisfaction.
🔹In this view, advertising enters the utility function directly – it's part of what consumers enjoy.

By contrast, complementary view of advertising holds that advertising does not attempt to persuade buyers to change their tastes.

🔹This view rejects the persuasive view's assumption that tastes change.
🔹Tastes are stable – advertising works through a different mechanism.
🔹Advertising is a complement, not a persuader.

As an example, it may be that a consumer values "social prestige", and advertising may then serve as an input that enables the consumer to derive more social prestige when the advertised product is consumed.

🔹Social prestige = status, respect from others.
🔹Advertising can make a product a symbol of prestige.
🔹The consumer buys both the product and the prestige.

This view is logically distinct from the persuasive view, since the complementary view: Holds that consumers possess a stable set of preferences into which advertising enters as one argument.

🔹Stable preferences = no change in underlying tastes.
🔹Advertising is an argument in the utility function – one of many inputs to satisfaction.
🔹This allows welfare analysis using standard economics.

Under this view, the market may provide too little advertising, since the advertising firm does not internalize the full increase in consumer surplus that its advertising engenders.

🔹Too little advertising = less than socially optimal.
🔹Internalize = the firm captures the benefit.
🔹Advertising creates consumer surplus, but the firm cannot charge for it – so it under-invests.
📌 Complementary View: Advertising adds value directly to the consumption experience (e.g., prestige, status). With stable preferences, the market may provide too little advertising.

Optimal Level of Advertising or Measuring Advertising Intensity

Optimal Level of Advertising or Measuring Advertising Intensity – To what extents we have to advertise our product?

🔹This asks the key managerial question: How much should we spend on advertising?
🔹Not too little (missed opportunities) and not too much (wasted money).
🔹The answer depends on costs, benefits, and market structure.

In imperfectly competitive markets, competition between firms is based on using a combination of advertising, price and product characteristics.

🔹Imperfectly competitive markets = monopoly, oligopoly, monopolistic competition.
🔹Firms have multiple competitive tools: advertising, price, quality, features, service.

To maximize profits a firm will equate marginal revenue to marginal cost whether it advertises or not.

🔹Profit maximization rule – MR = MC applies to all decisions.
🔹The firm should advertise until the last dollar of advertising brings in one dollar of extra profit.

The optimal level of advertising expenditure for the firm is determined where the marginal increase in costs of advertising is equal to the marginal increase in revenue.

🔹Marginal increase in costs = additional cost of one more ad.
🔹Marginal increase in revenue = additional sales revenue from that ad.
🔹Optimal point: ΔC = ΔR from advertising.

The effect of advertisement on consumer preference – Advertising expenditure may have an impact on consumer preferences and sales in more than one period.

🔹More than one period = current advertising affects future sales (carryover effect).
🔹Consumers may remember an ad for months or years.
🔹This is called the advertising capital or brand equity concept.

Advertising in one period, therefore, can have an impact on sales in future periods because advertising builds continued awareness of the product or firm among consumers.

🔹Continued awareness = the brand stays in consumers' minds over time.
🔹Each ad contributes to a "stock" of brand awareness.

Giving consideration to future impacts would justify higher levels of advertising in the initial period than the single period analysis might suggest.

🔹Future impacts = sales in later periods caused by today's ads.
🔹The simple model ignores future benefits → underestimates advertising's value.
📌 Optimal Advertising Rule: Advertise until marginal cost = marginal revenue from advertising. Future carryover effects justify higher spending than single-period analysis suggests.

Advertising and Market Structure

Advertising and Market Structure – What matters the firm's extents of advertisement expenditure? One of the factors determining the level of a firm's advertising expenditure is the size and number of competitors in the market.

🔹Market structure influences advertising intensity.

If the firm sells a homogeneous product in a perfectly competitive market, then advertising would appear to be unnecessary.

🔹Homogeneous product = identical products from all firms.
🔹If all products are identical and consumers know it, why advertise?

At the other extreme, a monopolist would likewise hardly need to advertise because consumers would have no other source of the product.

🔹Monopolist = only seller of the product.
🔹If consumers want the product, they have to buy from the monopolist.

Therefore, in competitive and duopoly market structure with product differentiation, advertising might be expected to be a significant competitive weapon.

🔹Product differentiation = products are not identical.
🔹In such markets, advertising is powerful – it differentiates further and builds loyalty.

In monopolistically competitive markets, products are differentiated; each firm's product is not a perfect substitute for the products of other suppliers.

🔹Monopolistic competition = many firms, differentiated products (restaurants, clothing).
🔹Advertising reinforces differentiation and increases perceived uniqueness.

The demand for each firm's product tends to be more price-inelastic than in more competitive markets and the advertising-to-sales ratio would be higher.

🔹More price-inelastic = less responsive to price changes.
🔹Advertising-to-sales ratio = advertising spending as a share of revenue.

If a firm gains an increased market share in a rapidly expanding market by advertising, then it will experience growth.

🔹Rapidly expanding market = demand growing fast.
🔹Advertising can capture a larger share of that growth.

Having achieved higher profits through increasing its advertising-to-sales ratio, the firm may continue to increase its ratio because it makes life difficult for its less successful rivals to maintain their position.

🔹Once a firm has high profits, it can reinvest in advertising.
🔹Higher advertising spending makes rivals' positions harder.
📌 Key Insight: Advertising intensity is highest in imperfectly competitive markets with product differentiation (monopolistic competition and oligopoly). Low in perfect competition and monopoly.

The Process of Innovation: Concepts and Relationships

The process of innovation: Concepts and Relationships – The terminology of innovation consists of a set of interrelated terms.

🔹The key terms are: invention, innovation, diffusion – they form a logical sequence.

The first and perhaps the most important one is the concept of invention. An invention is the creation of the new technology.

🔹Invention is the starting point – the creation of new knowledge.
🔹Creation = the act of conceiving or discovering.
🔹Many inventions never become innovations.

Cont'd – Technology refers to any tool or technique, product or process, physical equipment or method of doing or making; which extended human capability.

🔹Broad definition of technology: anything that extends what humans can do.
🔹Tools, techniques, products, processes, methods – all are technology.

The process of adopting an invention in a practical use is called innovation.

🔹Adopting in practical use = turning the invention into something useful.
🔹Innovation is the first commercialization or first practical application.
🔹Invention = idea. Innovation = reality.

If the existing product line is changed by a firm, i.e. it introduces a new product with or without displacement of the old ones, and then it is defined as product-innovation.

🔹Product innovation = new or improved product.
🔹Example: iPhone – displaced PDAs and some phones.

If a new method is initiated to produce existing products, then it is process innovation.

🔹Process innovation = new way of making the same product.
🔹Example: Henry Ford's assembly line – same Model T, made faster and cheaper.

According to Joseph Schumpeter there are five types of innovations. These are; The introduction of a new good, The introduction of a new method of production, Opening of a new market, The conquest of a new source of supply of raw materials, and Carrying out of the new organization

🔹Joseph Schumpeter (1883-1950) – father of innovation theory.
🔹Five types – broader than just product and process.
🔹This is the classic Schumpeterian definition of innovation.

Another useful concept related to the innovation process is 'imitation'. It is a situation when an innovation is copied by others or 'diffusion of the innovation'.

🔹Imitation = copying an innovation after it appears.
🔹Imitation spreads the innovation across the industry – this is diffusion.

The Schumpeterian series divided the process of technological change into three distinct phases. These are invention, innovation and diffusion

🔹Invention – the idea is born.
🔹Innovation – the idea is first commercialized.
🔹Diffusion – the innovation spreads across the market.
📌 Schumpeter's Three Phases: INVENTION → INNOVATION → DIFFUSION. Invention creates new knowledge; innovation commercializes it; diffusion spreads it across the market.
